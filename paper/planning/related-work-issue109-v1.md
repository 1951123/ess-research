## 8. Related Work

The established literature already covers workload-aware selection of statistics, interactions among statistical views, and choosing among alternative ways to exploit available summaries. Consequently, our point of differentiation is **not** the existence of several useful statistics, resource interactions, or alternative cardinality-estimation decompositions. We study a *resource-selection decision under incomplete information about the estimator's subsequent application strategy*: the advisor installs an unordered set \(M\), that set induces a family of complete admissible estimation schemes \(\Gamma_i(M)\) for each task, and the advisor's information about the estimator's choice within those families determines the relevant selection-risk functional. The general formulation makes no assumption that the advisor controls the applied scheme.

### 8.1 Statistics management and workload-driven design advisors

Automatic statistics selection has a substantial history. Chaudhuri and Narasayya's *Automating Statistics Management for Query Optimizers* (ICDE 2000) seeks a small set of essential statistics that preserves an optimizer's plan or estimated-cost behavior relative to a larger candidate set. Its magic-number sensitivity analysis and shrinking-set techniques explicitly rely on the optimizer's response to statistics availability. Importantly, the paper **acknowledges that more than one essential set may exist**, and that the set obtained by shrinking can depend on the order in which statistics are tested. It also considers multicolumn candidates and the cost of building or updating statistics. The paper thus cannot be characterized as assuming that all installed statistics contribute independently or as overlooking optimizer-specific statistics usage. Its primary question concerns finding an essential available-statistics subset under a given optimizer/equivalence criterion, rather than evaluating a chosen resource set against a prescribed *family of uncertain complete estimator application schemes*.

Bruno and Chaudhuri's *Exploiting Statistics on Query Expressions for Optimization* (SIGMOD 2002) enlarges the candidate resource space to statistics on intermediate query expressions (SITs), explains their applicability through the optimizer's expression/view machinery, and gives a workload-driven technique for selecting a useful SIT subset without first materializing every candidate. This work is directly relevant to the selection and use of statistical resources, and it **already incorporates uncertainty/sensitivity in an advisor mechanism**. Specifically, §§4.2–4.3 construct *Min* and *Max* extreme cardinality/selectivity propagations under alternative hypothetical attribute-value distributions, compare resulting estimated execution-plan costs, and use both the cost spread and contributions to selectivity uncertainty to score candidate SITs. This is a substantive prior precedent for uncertainty-aware statistics recommendations. Its uncertainty concerns hypothetical distributions, resulting selectivities and optimizer cost sensitivity, rather than worst modeled loss over the complete estimator-application schemes allowed by an installed resource set when the downstream scheme-choice strategy is unknown to the advisor. Moreover, SITs summarize **intermediate query expressions**, whereas our PostgreSQL instantiation installs **single-table two-column MCV objects**; conceptual comparisons of decisions and information assumptions do not equate those physical resources.

A particularly important antecedent is El-Helw, Ilyas, and Zuzarte's *StatAdvisor: Recommending Statistical Views* (PVLDB 2(2), 2009). StatAdvisor recommends statistical views under a resource constraint using plan-based candidate enumeration and benefit analysis. **It explicitly models interdependence in the benefit of different views**: several views can be valuable together even when individual members do not change the chosen plan, so the advisor groups mutually useful views into statview-groups rather than simply assigning independent gains to every view. It also considers how views match optimizer expressions and how combinations of statistics influence plans. Hence ESS cannot claim to originate interaction-aware statistics recommendations. The distinction we investigate is narrower: rather than grouping installed resources according to their observed joint plan benefit, our formulation explicitly makes the family of *complete, mutually competing estimator-application schemes* depend on \(M\), and changes the advisor's risk objective according to what is known about the later scheme-selection policy.

Broader physical-design advisors, including Bruno and Chaudhuri's *Automatic Physical Database Tuning: A Relaxation-based Approach* (SIGMOD 2005), place statistics decisions in the larger context of constrained index/view/design optimization. Our add-only greedy implementation is not proposed as a novel globally optimal search algorithm. Nor do we assert that previous advisors lack ways to model optimizer behavior: these precedents use optimizer feedback, what-if evaluation, and dependencies in different forms. The question here is specifically what an advisor can defensibly optimize when **the estimator's eventual selection among admissible complete schemes is not specified to it**.

### 8.2 Choosing how available statistics are applied

The most direct conceptual comparison is Bruno and Chaudhuri's *Conditional Selectivity for Statistics on Query Expressions* (SIGMOD 2004). It **explicitly recognizes multiple, possibly conflicting SITs and multiple decompositions for estimating the same selectivity**. It introduces conditional-selectivity identities and dynamic programming to search estimation alternatives and choose an accurate decomposition, including ways to exploit multiple SITs together when conventional view matching cannot. Thus **exploration and ranking of alternative ways to apply installed statistics is prior art**; we do not present it as a new idea or imply that earlier work always chooses a single fixed statistic.

Chen, Huang, Wang, Salihoglu and Salem's *Accurate Summary-based Cardinality Estimation Through the Lens of Cardinality Estimation Graphs* (PVLDB 15(8), 2022) is a second, particularly close antecedent. A **cardinality estimation graph (CEG)** represents alternative complete formulas for summary-based query estimation as paths through intermediate subqueries. The authors explicitly observe that existing estimators may have *undefined or fixed* path-selection rules and examine alternative strategies, including minimum- and maximum-weight paths. Thus the existence of alternative complete estimation paths **and uncertainty or arbitrariness about their downstream selection are themselves prior art**. CEG studies how an **estimator** can organize and select summary-based formulas (notably for join/graph queries); it does not, in the formulation compared here, optimize a separate **budgeted upstream statistics-installation set** against uncertainty over the subsequent choice among the installed set's admissible complete schemes. CEG paths and the labeled maximal matchings in our limited PostgreSQL two-column MCV example are different mathematical representations and must not be identified with each other.

The separation of decision variables is decisive. Conditional-selectivity and CEG work develop *estimator-side representations and methods* for selecting among favorable decompositions/paths. The ESS advisor, in contrast, chooses **which statistics to make available** and, in the uncertain-strategy formulation, **does not get to select or assume the estimator-side decomposition for each task**. The resulting \(\Gamma_i(M)\) is a prescribed set of complete admissible schemes, not a claim that all schemes are physically attainable in every DBMS. A nominal objective assumes specified policy information; shared-global worst-case risk couples tasks through one common policy; task-wise pessimism instead maximizes over each task's admissible schemes independently. These distinctions are about **information and optimization under unknown downstream decisions**, not about discovering that decomposition alternatives exist or inventing a better estimator.

For a restricted pairwise-MCV instance, we model admissible schemes by all **labeled inclusion-wise maximal matchings** on task-applicable selected edges. This is a structural specialization of the general \(M \mapsto \Gamma_i(M)\) interface. A source-grounded correspondence under A1–A4 describes one PostgreSQL 16.14 clause-consumption path; it is not a statement that the conditional-selectivity decompositions above are matchings, or that maximal matchings capture all PostgreSQL estimation paths.

### 8.3 Cardinality-estimation methods and evidence quality

Better summaries and cardinality estimators address a related but different design lever. PostgreSQL 16 documents multivariate *dependencies*, *ndistinct* and *MCV* statistics, whose creation requests data collection at a later `ANALYZE`; our physical instance is confined to selected two-column MCV objects. We do **not** propose a new summary-construction technique or replacement estimator. Likewise, pessimistic cardinality estimators such as Zhang et al.'s *LpBound* (2025) construct mathematically guaranteed **upper bounds on query-result cardinality** from degree-sequence statistics. The word “pessimistic” in our work denotes worst **model loss across admissible applied schemes**. It does not make a one-sided cardinality bound or an unconditional physical Q-error guarantee.

Leis et al.'s *How Good Are Query Optimizers, Really?* (PVLDB 2015) disentangles estimation, costing and plan enumeration in realistic query optimization. Han et al.'s *Cardinality Estimation in DBMS: A Comprehensive Benchmark Evaluation* (PVLDB 2021/2022 volume) further explains why Q-error alone does not characterize final plan quality. Wang et al.'s *Are We Ready for Learned Cardinality Estimation?* (PVLDB 2021) supplies the publicly available AreCEL workload family. We reuse its official Train/Test workload splits, **not** its learned estimator or its full published environment. Our frozen Train objective is a structured absolute-log *model* loss; actual PostgreSQL held-out `Plan Rows` Q-error and any hypothetical global-order evaluation are separate observables. Neither alone establishes lower execution latency.

### 8.4 Robust query optimization and uncertainty sets

Robust query optimization also changes the preferred decision when some inputs are uncertain. Xiu, Agarwal, and Yang's *PARQO: Penalty-Aware Robust Plan Selection in Query Optimization* (PVLDB 2024) models uncertain selectivities and chooses **query execution plans** using workload-informed error models and plan-penalty metrics. Raychaudhury et al.'s *Coresets for Robust Query Optimization* (PACMMOD/PODS 2026) studies robust **plan candidate sets** under noisy cardinalities and workload distributions. These works are relevant to the importance of specifying uncertainty sets and the meaning of robustness, but they address a different decision object and uncertainty source: **plan choice under uncertain estimates**, rather than **statistics installation under unknown estimator-internal scheme application**. We do not claim a new general theory of robust optimization.

Even within ESS, “unknown strategy” does not inherently mean a randomized estimator. An unknown but deterministic implementation may induce a single actual scheme. When a global policy \(\theta\) is shared by tasks, the worst aggregate loss over \(\theta\) need not equal the sum of per-task worst losses. In the pairwise instance, a uniformly sampled strict global priority induces a distribution over task matchings according to order multiplicity; it is not uniform sampling of matchings. The task-wise PESS criterion is therefore **one risk objective under an explicit information assumption**, not a universally superior choice. Our physical order study treats uniform relative-OID order as a **counterfactual evaluation distribution**, not an observed PostgreSQL production probability law.

### 8.5 Positioning

Taken together, prior work establishes that (i) advisors choose and maintain workload-useful statistics; (ii) statistical objects can have interdependent benefits; (iii) estimators can choose among different ways of combining installed summaries; and (iv) robust planning can account for estimation uncertainty. **We take all four as precedents.** The question this paper isolates is the *advisor/estimator interface*: the **complete admissible scheme space itself changes with the installed resource set**, but the **advisor has only specified information about the rule that chooses an applied scheme**. That joint dependence changes the meaning of a resource-selection objective. PESS, labeled maximal matching and the restricted PostgreSQL OID/MCV example serve, respectively, as a risk-functional instance, a structural instance and systems evidence. We do not claim priority over alternative estimator decompositions, interaction-aware statistics advisors or robust query planning, and do not assert exhaustive absence of related approaches outside the inspected literature.

## Source verification and novelty-comparison ledger (editorial notes, not manuscript prose)

**Comparison rule.** For each prior work, ask separately: (a) who decides which statistics to install? (b) who decides which *complete* estimation method/subset/decomposition to apply? (c) does the recommendation optimize against multiple possible downstream application policies whose realized choice is unknown to the advisor? An interaction-aware advisor may answer (a) very well without addressing (c); an estimator-side decomposition search may answer (b) without solving (c). Do not substitute “no prior work considers overlap” for this comparison.

| Closest work | What the inspected source affirmatively addresses | Distinction to test and retain (qualified) |
|---|---|---|
| Chaudhuri–Narasayya 2000 | Essential statistics sets, optimizer plan/cost equivalence, multicolumn candidates, multiple possible essential sets, order-sensitive shrinking | No explicit resource-selection risk functional over an *unknown estimator application-policy family* identified in the inspected method |
| Bruno–Chaudhuri 2002 | Choice of workload-useful intermediate-expression SITs; **Min/Max extreme distribution/cardinality/selectivity assumptions and estimated plan-cost sensitivity** used to rank candidates (§§4.2–4.3) | This is already uncertainty-aware advisory; distinguish uncertain **selectivity/distribution and plan-cost response** from unknown **downstream estimator scheme application** and its taskwise loss |
| Bruno–Chaudhuri 2004 | **Multiple potentially conflicting SITs**, conditional-selectivity decompositions, DP search for an accurate estimator-side alternative | **Do not claim scheme alternatives as new**; distinguish estimator-controlled choice from advisor optimization under unknown downstream choice | 
| Chen et al. 2022 (CEG) | **Alternative complete summary-based estimate formulas as CEG paths**, with undefined/fixed choices in earlier estimators and studied path-selection strategies | **Do not claim complete paths or path-choice ambiguity as new**; distinguish estimator-side path selection from upstream budgeted statistics choice under unknown downstream strategy; CEG paths are not MCV matchings |
| El-Helw–Ilyas–Zuzarte 2009 (StatAdvisor) | **Dependencies/interactions among statviews**, joint statview-groups, plan-based benefits, constrained recommendation | **Do not claim independent benefits in prior advisors**; distinguish plan-dependent group benefit from explicit admissible complete scheme families and strategy-information-based risk |
| PARQO 2024; robust plan coresets 2026 | Uncertain cardinality/selectivity and robust **plans** / plan families | Different decision object and source of uncertainty; neither is a direct baseline for frozen statistics sets without a redesigned study |
| LpBound 2025 | Statistical cardinality **upper bound** | One-sided data/result-size guarantee is not worst loss over estimator schemes |
