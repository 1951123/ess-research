\begin{abstract}
An advisor chooses one global, unordered set of estimation resources, while
the estimator selects a complete scheme for each task. We ask when an advisor
can make budgeted decisions knowing or prescribing the nonempty family of
admissible complete schemes, but lacking a sufficiently predictable
selection policy. Admissibility does not determine the estimate or loss of
each counterfactual scheme: these require an estimator oracle, measurements,
or an explicit approximation. We distinguish a known nominal policy, one
unknown shared policy, and a task-wise pessimistic risk criterion, retaining
an arbitrary nonadditive maintenance function. For a restricted PostgreSQL
16.14 setting with eligible built two-column most-common-value statistics,
we instantiate admissible schemes as maximal matchings under stated source
and fixture conditions. In the frozen rationalized Forest10 model, exact
Nominal and PESS optima separate at budget three, and this model-level
separation survives restriction to candidates with effective MCV payloads.
The displayed PESS deployment had one effective MCV payload versus three
for Nominal; their held-out error difference is descriptive and cannot
identify a policy effect. Larger Forest10 and Census13 valid-only models
have coincident Nominal and PESS optima at the tested budgets. These results
do not establish physical policy regret, real-scheme coverage,
maintenance-cost additivity, or improved query performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

When can knowledge of admissible complete estimation schemes, without
knowledge of the estimator's actual scheme-selection policy, support useful
budgeted resource decisions? This question concerns a boundary between an
advisor that chooses resources and an estimator that uses them. It requires
separating the admissibility of a scheme from both the policy that selects a
scheme and the estimate produced by that scheme.

These are distinct stages in a database setting. First, the advisor requests
an unordered set $M$ of candidate statistics. A requested object may be
created in the catalog, yet have no usable most-common-value (MCV) payload
after \texttt{ANALYZE}. Among payload-bearing objects, task predicates and
other eligibility rules determine which complete schemes are admissible; the
estimator's internal policy then selects one such scheme. Only after that
selection does the estimator produce a cardinality and a loss. PostgreSQL
documentation itself distinguishes creating an extended-statistics catalog
entry from collecting its data during \texttt{ANALYZE}
\cite{pgstatsdocs16}. Thus availability, payload, eligibility, selection,
and numerical outcome are not interchangeable. In the frozen Forest10
calibration, for example, requested $e_{01}$ had no built MCV payload, while
$e_{12},e_{23},e_{34}$ each had 300 MCV items \cite{issue23_forest10}.

For a path with candidate MCV edges $ab$, $bc$, and $cd$, if all three have
usable payloads and satisfy the restricted eligibility conditions, the
complete maximal matchings are $\{ab,cd\}$ and $\{bc\}$. A greedy selector
that continues until no eligible edge remains selects one of them according
to its priority rule. A PostgreSQL 16.14 trace fixture observed both outcomes
under two object orders \cite{pgtrace2026,pgsource1614}; this supports only
the restricted instance, not arbitrary queries or all outcomes in one fixed
catalog.

The advisor may know or defensibly approximate the rules defining a
nonempty family $\Gamma_i(M)$ of complete admissible schemes without knowing
the policy $\theta$ that selects among them. These are separate information
questions: a deterministic policy may be outside the advisor's control but
still known and predictable, in which case Nominal is justified; an unknown
policy shared across tasks motivates Joint-Robust; and when only task-wise
admissibility is trusted, PESS is a conservative risk criterion. PESS does
not assert that PostgreSQL acts adversarially or that the task-wise worst
choices can occur under one shared policy. A known distribution could instead
motivate expected loss.

Knowing $\Gamma_i(M)$ alone does not reveal the estimate or loss of every
counterfactual scheme. An executable optimization instance also needs
scheme-wise losses, supplied by an estimator oracle, measurements, or an
explicit approximation. Our generic formulation states this input
requirement; the frozen numerical instance multiplies measured singleton
corrections over an admissible matching. The archived composition check does
not establish fidelity for these matching-conditioned counterfactual losses.
The generic model retains an arbitrary nonnegative task loss and a
nonadditive maintenance function.

We make four bounded contributions. First, we define one global unordered
resource decision, distinguish prescribed families $\Gamma_i(M)$ from
reachable schemes $\mathcal R_i(M)$, and state the policy information
required by Nominal, Joint-Robust, and PESS.
Second, we instantiate $\Gamma_i(M)$ as maximal matchings for a restricted
PostgreSQL 16.14 pairwise-MCV path and report source-derived and fixture-level
evidence for that correspondence. Third, we give model-internal decision
comparisons: a conflict-free singleton-family condition is an elementary
sufficient certificate for coincident optima, and the frozen six-task
decision separation persists when the candidate universe is restricted to
the three resources with effective MCV payloads. Fourth, we report both
policy-sensitive and policy-insensitive frozen model cases alongside
separately scoped physical observations. These claims have stated evidence
boundaries: conflict alone does not determine policy sensitivity, and PESS
is not shown to improve PostgreSQL outcomes.

Our falsifiable evidence claim is that policy information can change the
model-selected resource set in some frozen inputs but not others: the exact
six-task Forest10 instance separates Nominal and PESS at $B=3$, including
after restriction to effective-MCV candidates, while the larger Forest10 and
Census13 valid-only models have coincident optima for tested $B\leq3$.
These are model-level comparisons, not a claim that physical policy effects
were measured. In the held-out six-task comparison, the displayed PESS set
$\{e_{01},e_{34}\}$ had only $e_{34}$ with an effective payload, versus
three payloads for the Nominal set; its $+0.024223$ mean pre-clamp loss
difference over 60 query IDs and two repetitions is descriptive, not causal.
The 47.49\% and 46.27\% rates count raw complete candidate graphs with at
least two maximal matchings, not PostgreSQL catalogs with multiple built and
eligible statistics. Strict syntax-eligible cohorts contain 135 and 58 of
10,000 queries; their intersections with raw conflicts and realized
PostgreSQL ambiguity are unknown \cite{issue38_heldout,issue59_structure,
issue64_forest10,issue71_census13}.

The paper's main empirical question is when policy knowledge changes the
selected resource set, conditional on defensible schemes and loss estimates. Prior work already covers automatic statistics selection,
statistics composition, robust query optimization, and decision-dependent
uncertainty; we do not claim a new generic robust-optimization framework or
a first statistics advisor. Whether the specific database coupling here is
sufficiently distinctive for a regular research paper remains unresolved.
The remaining sections define the interfaces and information regimes,
present the restricted PostgreSQL instance and model analysis, and separate
frozen model results from PostgreSQL observations.

\section{Related Work}

Automatic statistics management is the closest resource-selection precedent.
Chaudhuri and Narasayya select essential base-table statistics according to
workload and the behavior of a specified optimizer
\cite{chaudhuri_narasayya_2000}. Bruno and Chaudhuri's SIGMOD 2002 work is
closer still: it selects a space-bounded subset of statistics on query
expressions, using extreme hypothetical selectivities and optimizer plans to
estimate which statistics matter \cite{bruno_chaudhuri_2002}. In both cases
the advisor controls which statistics are created, while a concrete optimizer
determines how the resulting information affects estimates and plans.
Statistics availability changes the optimizer's possible inputs and plan
responses, but these papers do not model an externally unknown policy over a
resource-dependent family of complete estimation schemes. Thus ESS cannot
claim novelty for resource selection under estimated benefits or hypothetical
estimation scenarios; its proposed distinction is the explicit information
boundary between a prescribed scheme family and a possibly unknown selector.

Bruno and Chaudhuri's SIGMOD 2004 work directly studies the estimator-use
layer: a dynamic program reasons over conditional selectivities to find an
accurate estimate from available query-expression statistics
\cite{bruno_chaudhuri_2004}. MAXENT is an even closer semantic predecessor
to the observation that partial multivariate statistics may admit
non-equivalent estimation methods; it chooses a consistent maximum-entropy
model for using that information \cite{markl_et_al_2006}. Both make
estimator-side combination explicit and resource availability affects the
combinations or constraints available to their estimator. Their estimator
choice is designed as part of the method, however, whereas ESS leaves the
internal selection policy outside the advisor and asks how resource decisions
depend on what that advisor knows about admissible schemes. This distinction
does not make the existence of multiple estimates or combinations a new
contribution.

Robust query optimization and physical design address other decision layers.
Babcock and Chaudhuri use Bayesian cardinality estimates from samples to
trade query-plan performance against predictability
\cite{babcock_chaudhuri_2005}. El Gebaly and Aboulnaga choose physical
designs while accounting for estimated-benefit errors and workload changes;
their PostgreSQL implementation evaluates risk and generality of designs
\cite{elgebaly_aboulnaga_2008}. These works control plans or physical
structures and model uncertain cardinalities, costs, or workloads, rather
than uncertainty over complete estimator schemes induced by an advisor's
statistics set. Nohadani and Sharma formalize robust optimization with
decision-dependent uncertainty sets and study complexity and reformulations
\cite{nohadani_sharma_2018}. ESS uses that established mathematical setting
as context; neither decision-dependent uncertainty nor generic min--max
optimization is claimed as new here.

AutoCE is a useful recent advisor comparator. It chooses a learned
cardinality-estimator model for a dataset using a learned recommendation
model and integrates that advisor into PostgreSQL
\cite{autoce_icde2023}. In AutoCE, the advisor chooses the estimator model;
ESS instead chooses statistics resources while a separate estimator may
select a scheme from those resources. EDB's Statistics Advisor is a practical
PostgreSQL-facing resource-selection system: it monitors multi-column
filters, considers extended-statistics candidates, and weights candidates
using workload benefit and execution cost \cite{edb_statistics_advisor}.
PostgreSQL's documentation also distinguishes a statistics object's
catalog-level creation from data collection by \texttt{ANALYZE}
\cite{pgstatsdocs16}. These systems establish that statistics advising and
the requested-versus-built distinction are practical concerns; their
documentation does not specify the scheme-family and policy-uncertainty
model used here.

Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes, for each closest comparator, who controls
the resource or estimator choice, the uncertain quantity, whether resource
changes alter modeled outcomes, and the limited distinction ESS examines.
Together, these works substantially narrow the novelty claim. The present
manuscript contributes no new generic robust-optimization framework,
estimator-combination algorithm, or statistics advisor. It formulates one
specific external-advisor problem and reports finite model evidence under
stated scheme and loss inputs. Whether that coupling is sufficiently
distinctive for a regular research paper remains open.

\section{Model}

\subsection{Resources, Tasks, and Admissible Schemes}

Let $\mathcal I$ be a finite set of estimation tasks. Task $i$ has true
cardinality $N_i\geq 0$, reference target
$T_i=\max\{1,N_i\}$, and weight $w_i>0$. A task identifies the cardinality
target and its estimation context; it need not be an entire SQL query or a
physical plan node. Let $\E$ be a finite set of candidate estimation
resources. The advisor chooses one global, unordered resource set
$M\subseteq\E$, subject to a maintenance budget.

The generic maintenance function
\[
  \operatorname{Maint}:2^\E\longrightarrow\mathbb R_{\geq0},
  \qquad \operatorname{Maint}(\varnothing)=0
\]
is the additional maintenance cost relative to the baseline database
maintenance process. It may depend on fixed conditions $\xi$, such as
database state, data-modification workload, refresh policy, evaluation
horizon, and accounting convention. We write
$\operatorname{Maint}(M;\xi)$ when exposing those conditions and abbreviate
it as $\operatorname{Maint}(M)$ for a fixed instance. We require
$\operatorname{Maint}(M)\leq B$ for $B\geq0$. No additivity, monotonicity, or
submodularity is assumed.

For each task, let $\Omega_i$ be a finite universe of complete estimation
schemes. A scheme contains enough information to determine the estimate and
has a resource-use set $\operatorname{used}(\sigma)\subseteq\E$. The
scheme may encode selected resources, order, predicate coverage, and
estimator-specific context; it need not itself be a set or a plan. The
prescribed admissibility map is
\[
  \Gamma_i:2^\E\to 2^{\Omega_i}\setminus\{\varnothing\},
  \qquad
  \sigma\in\Gamma_i(M)\Longrightarrow\operatorname{used}(\sigma)\subseteq M.
\]
We require $\Gamma_i(M)\ne\varnothing$ for every task and created set.
There is a baseline scheme in $\Gamma_i(\varnothing)$, but it need not remain
admissible after resources are added. In particular, $\Gamma_i$ need not be
nested. The estimator is a positive-valued map
$\operatorname{Est}_i:\Omega_i\to\mathbb R_{>0}$ and the task loss is any
nonnegative map $\ell_i:\Omega_i\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}$. Our leading
illustration is absolute log error,
\[
  \ell_i(\sigma)=
  \left|\log\frac{\operatorname{Est}_i(\sigma)}{T_i}\right|.
\]
This expression is defined because $T_i\geq1$ and the modeled estimate is
positive. It is a pre-clamp convention; it does not identify PostgreSQL
\texttt{Plan Rows} with the raw estimator value.

The maps $\Gamma_i$, $\operatorname{Est}_i$, and $\ell_i$ are distinct
inputs to the optimization problem. An implementation must be able to
evaluate or query $\Gamma_i(M)$ for the resource sets it considers and must
obtain a loss for every admissible counterfactual scheme, or provide an
oracle that computes the required maxima. The admissibility rules can be
known or defensibly approximated without revealing the selector's policy;
that knowledge alone does not reveal an estimate for a scheme that has not
been measured or evaluated. Such losses therefore require an estimator
oracle, measurements, or an explicitly stated approximation. The finite
Forest10 model uses measured single-object corrections and an assumed
multiplicative composition rule. The archived overlap residuals show that
this rule is not a PostgreSQL identity, so the model optima are exact only
for the stored rationalized inputs, not for every physical counterfactual
estimate.


The generic estimator map has no assumed additive or multiplicative
structure across resources. Nor does the scheme universe have to consist of
resource subsets: a complete scheme may carry order, fallback, or estimator
state that affects the output even when its selected resource set is the
same. The resource-use consistency condition only says that a scheme cannot
use an uncreated candidate; it does not say that every created candidate is
used or that two schemes with the same used set have the same estimate.

For comparison, let $\mathcal R_i(M)$ denote the set of complete schemes the
deployed system can actually produce under the evaluation conditions. This
real set is an empirical or source-dependent object, not part of the generic
input unless an application supplies it. The formulation requires no
containment between $\mathcal R_i(M)$ and $\Gamma_i(M)$. If every real
outcome is represented by a scheme in $\Gamma_i(M)$ and the same loss is
used, the model's task-wise maximum upper-bounds the real worst-case loss.
Without that inclusion and aligned measurements, PESS is a model objective;
it is not a database guarantee. Equality would be a stronger claim still.

If each SQL query contributes exactly one task, the task set is
query-indexed. A query may instead contribute several logical
cardinality-estimation tasks. A task specifies the target cardinality, a
scheme specifies how available resources and estimator choices produce
that target, and a physical plan specifies execution; these objects are
distinct.

The pessimistic selection problem is
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
  \min_{M\subseteq\E:\operatorname{Maint}(M)\leq B}\quad
    &F_{\Pess}(M),\\
  F_{\Pess}(M)&=
    \sum_{i\in\mathcal I}w_i
    \max_{\sigma\in\Gamma_i(M)}\ell_i(\sigma).
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{1}
\]
The sum of task-wise maxima need not be attained by one shared policy.
Also, if $\mathcal R_i(M)$ denotes the real set of schemes the DBMS can
produce, no relation between $\mathcal R_i(M)$ and $\Gamma_i(M)$ is implicit.
PESS bounds a real worst-case loss only under an appropriate containment
$\mathcal R_i(M)\subseteq\Gamma_i(M)$ and aligned estimator/loss assumptions.

\subsection{Three Information Regimes}

Suppose a policy parameter $\theta$ induces a complete scheme
$\sigma_i(M;\theta)\in\Gamma_i(M)$ for each task and created set. Let
$\Theta$ be a finite nonempty family of globally shared policies, and let
$\theta_0\in\Theta$ be a known nominal policy. For the same losses and budget
define
\[
\begin{aligned}
F_{\Nom}(M)&=\sum_i w_i\ell_i(\sigma_i(M;\theta_0)),\\
F_{\Joint}(M)&=\max_{\theta\in\Theta}
  \sum_i w_i\ell_i(\sigma_i(M;\theta)),\\
F_{\Pess}(M)&=\sum_i w_i\max_{\sigma\in\Gamma_i(M)}\ell_i(\sigma).
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
Nominal uses a policy mapping that is sufficiently known and credible for
the advisor's counterfactual resource sets. A policy may be deterministic and
outside the advisor's control yet still be known; lack of control alone does
not justify uncertainty. Joint assumes one unknown policy parameter is shared
across tasks, with a specified candidate family $\Theta$. PESS assumes only
the task-specific admissibility families are justified and applies
independent task-wise maxima. It is a conservative risk criterion, not a
claim that a DBMS behaves adversarially or that all task maxima are jointly
realizable. If a choice distribution is known and expected quality is the
target, an expectation-based criterion may be preferable. Whenever every policy outcome lies in $\Gamma_i(M)$ and
$\theta_0\in\Theta$, the fixed-set hierarchy
$F_{\Nom}(M)\leq F_{\Joint}(M)\leq F_{\Pess}(M)$ follows. It compares the
same set and modeled estimator, not the separately optimized sets under
actual PostgreSQL outcomes.

The three quantities encode different information available at decision
time. Nominal assumes the advisor knows or can predict the reference policy
$\theta_0$ across candidate resource sets. Joint assumes it knows the finite
policy family $\Theta$ but not which single member will govern all tasks.
PESS does not require a common policy parameter; it uses only the per-task
envelopes $\Gamma_i(M)$ together with their separately supplied scheme
losses. The hierarchy is
pointwise, before minimizing over $M$. Consequently, the values of the
separately selected solutions need not be ordered when each is evaluated
under a different objective, and the PESS optimizer need not minimize
Nominal loss. The two-task example in Section~\ref{sec:decision-analysis}
will show why the shared and task-wise maxima can differ strictly.

\subsection{Pairwise Maximal-Matching Instance}

For the PostgreSQL instance, each candidate $e\in\E_i$ is a built
two-column MCV object over two distinct attributes. It is represented as a
labeled edge in a graph $H_i=(V_i,\E_i)$; different objects with the same
endpoints remain distinct parallel edges. For a created set $M$,
$H_i[M]$ contains edges in $M\cap\E_i$. A matching is a set of edges with no
shared endpoint. We prescribe
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \Omega_i^{\mathrm{MM}}&=\operatorname{Matchings}(H_i),\\
  \Gamma_i^{\mathrm{MM}}(M)&=
    \operatorname{MaximalMatchings}(H_i[M]).
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]
Maximal means that no available edge can be added; it does not mean
maximum-cardinality. Every finite graph has a maximal matching, and an
edgeless graph has the unique empty matching, so (3) is nonempty for all
$M$. This is a structured instance of (1), not the generic interface.

\section{PostgreSQL Instantiation}

\subsection{When the Selector Is a Greedy Matching Procedure}

The PostgreSQL correspondence is conditional on PostgreSQL 16.14 and a
restricted single-relation AND path. Each relevant clause must be a
supported, simple predicate on one distinct attribute, with exactly one
relevant clause per attribute. Every candidate must be a built two-column
MCV object on that relation, and both of its attribute clauses must remain
eligible for the MCV path. We exclude joins, OR expressions, expression
keys, repeated attributes, multiple relevant clauses on one attribute,
permission exclusions, and other estimators that pre-consume a clause. Under
these assumptions, all currently eligible two-key objects tie on newly
covered attributes and width. The pinned source resolves that tie by
candidate-list order; the list is normally ordered by statistics-object OID.
It then marks the selected clauses consumed and repeats until no candidate
remains \cite{pgsource1614}.

Under this gate, each selected statistic contributes two distinct attribute
vertices. A later eligible statistic can be selected only while both of its
attributes remain available to the MCV estimation path; any shared endpoint
would have been consumed by the earlier selection. The source loop therefore
acts as a priority-ordered greedy procedure on the candidate graph. Its
tie-breaking order selects one matching for a fixed catalog state. The
abstract matching family in (3) ranges over multiple orders to represent
uncertainty about that choice; it should not be read as a randomized runtime
policy or as empirical evidence about the frequency of any matching.

\begin{proposition}[Restricted selector correspondence]
Under the preceding assumptions, the statistics selected by one fixed
PostgreSQL candidate order form a maximal matching of $H_i[M]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each selected two-column object consumes two distinct clauses. A later
selected object must therefore be disjoint from every earlier one, so the
selected edges form a matching. If an available edge were disjoint from that
matching at termination, both of its clauses would remain unconsumed and it
would still be eligible, contradicting termination. Thus the matching is
maximal.
\end{proof}

Under an arbitrary abstract total priority order, every maximal matching can
be obtained by placing its edges before the other edges.

\begin{proposition}[Abstract order completeness]
Under the same eligibility assumptions, the set of outcomes over all
abstract total priority orders is exactly
$\operatorname{MaximalMatchings}(H_i[M])$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The selector correspondence proves that each outcome is a maximal
matching. Conversely, fix a maximal matching $P$ and order its edges before
all other available edges. Each edge of $P$ remains eligible until selected
because $P$ has disjoint endpoints. After its edges have been selected,
maximality implies that every remaining edge meets a covered endpoint, so
the selector stops with exactly $P$.
\end{proof}

Order completeness is a mathematical statement about a freely varying
abstract priority order. It does not say that arbitrary orders are
realizable by changing OIDs in one fixed catalog, or that PostgreSQL's real
possible-scheme set equals (3).

Figure~\ref{fig:path} shows the narrow runtime fixture. An instrumented
PostgreSQL 16.14 build traced selected OIDs and consumed clause indices,
observing $\{ab,cd\}$ in one creation order and $\{bc\}$ in another
\cite{pgtrace2026}. A separate equality-only gate covered path, star,
disconnected, and parallel-edge cases: 26 observations were within the
source-derived gate with no counterexample, two were outside it because an
MCV payload was absent, and four empty-resource observations were
inconclusive because the trace loop was not entered \cite{pg_gate2026}. These
results support the stated scope; they are not universal PostgreSQL evidence.

\subsection{Positive Pre-Clamp Loss and Maintenance}

For a task with positive baseline pre-clamp estimate $n_i^0$ and positive
single-object correction factors $r_{e,i}$, the model assumes
\[
  \operatorname{Est}^{\mathrm{mult}}_i(P)
    =n_i^0\prod_{e\in P}r_{e,i},\quad
  \alpha_i=\log(n_i^0/T_i),\quad b_{e,i}=\log r_{e,i},
\]
and hence
\[
  \ell_i^{\mathrm{raw}}(P)=
  \left|\alpha_i+\sum_{e\in P}b_{e,i}\right|.
\tag{4}
\]
This is an explicit multiplicative-separability assumption, not an
identity for PostgreSQL. A bounded Forest10 composition check found near
agreement for one disjoint pair but relative residuals $0.1590$ and
$-0.4904$ for two overlapping pairs \cite{issue23_forest10}. The log loss
also requires positive pre-clamp estimates. PostgreSQL's finalized
\texttt{Plan Rows} applies rounding and clamping, so its error is a separate
metric.

For the ordinary finite-positive PostgreSQL regime, the source-scoped
conversion can be represented as
\[
\begin{aligned}
 C_{\mathrm{PG}}(x)&=
 \begin{cases}
  1,&0<x\leq1,\\
  \operatorname{rint}(x),&1<x\leq x_{\max},
 \end{cases}\\
 \ell_i^{\mathrm{plan}}(P)&=
  \left|\log C_{\mathrm{PG}}(\operatorname{Est}^{\mathrm{mult}}_i(P))\right.\\[-2pt]
 &\qquad\left.-\log T_i\right|.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]
Here $x_{\max}$ is the implementation's finite cap and
$\operatorname{rint}$ follows its floating-point rounding mode. Nonfinite
inputs and separate very-large-value paths are outside this expression.
Raw and plan loss need not have the same optimizer. We use raw
pre-clamp loss for the theoretical model and report finalized Plan Rows only
as a separate observed outcome.

\subsection{Maintenance Cost and Experimental Proxies}

The abstract $\operatorname{Maint}(M)$ in (1) remains nonadditive. For a
controlled PostgreSQL instance, one possible period-level proxy is
\[
  \widehat{\operatorname{Maint}}^{\mathrm{PG}}(M)
     =\sum_{e\in M}\bar m_e,\qquad
  \bar m_e=\nu_{t(e)}p_e(S_{t(e)}),
\tag{6}
\]
where $p_e(S_t)$ denotes candidate-specific CPU work for refreshing object
$e$ from a fixed reference sample of table $t$, and $\nu_t$ is an externally
specified refresh count over the evaluation horizon. This expression is a
model, not an empirical identity. Candidate-specific construction work,
serialization, and attributable catalog work would need separate
measurement; shared sample acquisition and baseline single-column
maintenance are outside the proxy.

Equal positive statistics targets can keep the source-derived requested
sampling requirement unchanged across candidate sets, but they do not force
repeated \texttt{ANALYZE} runs to realize identical random samples. Holding
the tuple values and estimated relation size fixed is a stronger experimental
control; even that does not prove additive CPU time. The coefficients in (6)
have not been calibrated in the reported selection studies. Instead, the
selection experiments use $\bar m_e=1$ and
$B\in\{0,2,3\}$, so the empirical constraint is an object-count proxy.
We report no physical maintenance cost or latency result.

\section{Decision Analysis}
\label{sec:decision-analysis}

\subsection{Pointwise Bounds and a Conflict-Free Certificate}

The hierarchy in (2) follows directly from set inclusion. For a fixed $M$,
the nominal scheme is one member of the shared family, and each shared-policy
scheme is in $\Gamma_i(M)$. Therefore,
\[
F_{\Nom}(M)\leq F_{\Joint}(M)\leq F_{\Pess}(M).
\tag{7}
\]
The inequalities do not order the actual outcomes of the separately selected
sets, and they do not establish a real-system guarantee when the prescribed
families are misspecified.

\begin{lemma}[Conflict-free nominal optimum]
Let $M_{\Nom}$ be a global minimizer of $F_{\Nom}$ over the budget-feasible
sets. If every $\Gamma_i(M_{\Nom})$ is a singleton containing the nominal
scheme, then $M_{\Nom}$ is also a global minimizer of $F_{\Joint}$ and
$F_{\Pess}$, and all three objective values agree at $M_{\Nom}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The singleton condition gives equality of all three objectives at
$M_{\Nom}$. For any feasible $M$, the fixed-set hierarchy and nominal
optimality give
\[
  F_{\Pess}(M)\geq F_{\Joint}(M)\geq F_{\Nom}(M)
  \geq F_{\Nom}(M_{\Nom}).
\]
Thus $M_{\Nom}$ is optimal for both conservative objectives as well.
\end{proof}

In the matching instance it suffices that each $H_i[M_{\Nom}]$ is itself a
matching: its only maximal matching is the set of all its edges (including
the empty case). This is a model-internal sufficient certificate. It does
not say that optimal sets will usually be conflict-free or that every PG
catalog realizes every modeled scheme.
Within this instance, conflict permits multiple maximal matchings but
does not ensure different losses or different optimal resource sets. In the
generic interface, complete schemes can also differ by order or estimator
context without a graph-edge conflict. Thus edge conflict alone is neither
a sufficient nor a generally necessary test for policy-sensitive decisions.

\subsection{Active-MCV-Only Restriction of the Frozen Model}

The exact Issue~\#23 model at budget $B=3$ has the unique Nominal optimum
$M_N=\{e_{12},e_{23},e_{34}\}$. Its four tied PESS optima are
\[
\begin{aligned}
\arg\min_{M\subseteq\E:\,|M|\leq3}F_{\Pess}(M)
=\{&\{e_{01},e_{34}\},\{e_{01},e_{34},e_{56}\},\\
&\{e_{34}\},\{e_{34},e_{56}\}\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
At $M_N$, the full-universe PESS value is
$14.320233961846\ldots$; the optimum is
$13.498446365277\ldots$. The strict gap is approximately
$0.821787596569$. These decimals summarize the exact Fraction comparison
recorded for the frozen inputs \cite{issue23_forest10}.

\begin{proposition}[Restriction to candidates with effective MCV payloads]
Restrict the allowed candidate universe to
$A=\{e_{12},e_{23},e_{34}\}$, the only three objects with effective
300-item MCV payloads in the frozen calibration. Hold fixed the six task
coefficients, original relative nominal priority, maximal-matching
admissibility rule, losses, unit costs, and budget $B=3$. Then $M_N$
remains the unique Nominal optimum; $\{e_{34}\}$ is the unique PESS optimum
in the restricted universe; and $M_N$ remains strictly suboptimal for PESS.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every $M\subseteq A$, its induced task graphs, admissible schemes, and
losses are unchanged from the full-universe model, since no removed
candidate is installed. Costs and feasibility are unchanged as well. Thus
the full-universe Nominal optimum remains the unique optimum on the
restricted domain. The original full-universe PESS optimum set has four
members listed above; only $\{e_{34}\}$ lies in $2^A$. Since the archived
exact enumeration lists all ties, it is the unique restricted PESS optimum.
The PESS value and strict gap at $M_N$ are unchanged.
\end{proof}

This restriction-inheritance argument is a logical corollary of archived
exact results, not a newly executed enumeration or theorem of independent
novelty. It shows that the model-level decision separation does not depend
solely on retaining candidates without effective payloads. It does not
predict PostgreSQL numeric outcomes or establish a physical policy effect.

\subsection{Why Shared and Task-Wise Robustness Differ}

Consider two tasks and two overlapping resources $e,f$. With both resources
created, the maximal matchings are $\{e\}$ and $\{f\}$. Let losses for task
1 under $(e,f)$ be $(1,0)$ and for task 2 be $(0,1)$, with unit weights. A
single shared priority selects the same edge for both tasks, so
$F_{\Joint}(\{e,f\})=1$. Independent task-wise maximization selects the
harmful edge separately for each task, giving
$F_{\Pess}(\{e,f\})=2$. Thus PESS is an envelope over task-wise admissible
schemes, not necessarily a realizable global policy.

The dependence of $\Gamma_i$ on $M$ is also necessary for a nontrivial
worst-case selection objective.

\begin{proposition}[Nested-family degeneracy]
Suppose, for every task $i$, that
$\Gamma_i(M)\subseteq\Gamma_i(M')$ whenever $M\subseteq M'$. Then
$F_{\Pess}(M)\leq F_{\Pess}(M')$. Consequently, the empty set is optimal
for every budget $B\geq0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For each task, the maximum of a nonnegative loss over the smaller family
cannot exceed its maximum over the larger family. Multiplying by $w_i>0$
and summing gives monotonicity. Since
$\operatorname{Maint}(\varnothing)=0\leq B$, the empty set is feasible and
has value no greater than any feasible set.
\end{proof}

Maximal-matching families need not be nested: adding an edge can make an old
complete matching non-maximal. For example, with one available edge $e_{ab}$,
the unique admissible matching is $\{e_{ab}\}$. Add a disjoint edge
$e_{cd}$; the old singleton is no longer maximal, because $e_{cd}$ can be
added, and the new admissible family contains only $\{e_{ab},e_{cd}\}$.
Whether this change raises or lowers the worst loss depends on the task's
losses. Thus the matching instance can violate the nesting premise behind the
degeneracy result, but it does not imply that every resource addition helps
or that every instance has a useful nonempty optimum.

\subsection{Loss Geometry and Inherited Hardness}

For a fixed task and created set, let
\[
  \mathcal M_i(M)=\operatorname{MaximalMatchings}(H_i[M])
\]
and define
\[
  U_i(M)=\max_{P\in\mathcal M_i(M)}\sum_{e\in P}b_{e,i},
  \qquad
  L_i(M)=\min_{P\in\mathcal M_i(M)}\sum_{e\in P}b_{e,i}.
\]
The task's worst raw loss has the endpoint form
\[
  \max_{P\in\mathcal M_i(M)}
  \left|\alpha_i+\sum_{e\in P}b_{e,i}\right|
  =
  \max\{\alpha_i+U_i(M),-\alpha_i-L_i(M)\}.
\tag{8}
\]
This follows from $|x|=\max\{x,-x\}$ and finiteness of
$\mathcal M_i(M)$. The two extrema are over maximal matchings, not ordinary
maximum-weight or maximum-cardinality matchings.

Exact evaluation can inherit the known hardness of minimum maximal
matching \cite{yannakakis_gavril_1980}. Let a graph have $m\geq1$ edges,
make all edges available to one task, and set $b_e=1$ and $\alpha=-m$.
Every maximal matching $P$ has $|P|\leq m$, so its loss is $m-|P|$.
Maximizing the loss therefore computes the minimum cardinality of a maximal
matching. This is a fixed-$M$ hardness observation; it is not a new graph
result or a general classification of the estimator-agnostic problem.

Outer selection can also remain difficult when there is no scheme
uncertainty. With vertex-disjoint candidate edges, every selected set has
one maximal matching and raw loss
$|\alpha+\sum_{e\in M}b_e|$. If integer coefficients are represented
directly, setting $\alpha=-T$, $b_{e_j}=a_j$, and zero maintenance costs
reduces SUBSET SUM on positive integers $a_j$ and target $T$ to deciding
whether some selection has zero loss. This is a representation-scoped
weak-hardness construction: it does not establish a polynomial-time
reduction for every encoding through measured multipliers $r_{e,i}$, and it
says nothing about physical PostgreSQL instances.

\subsection{Computational Scope}

The generic interfaces permit arbitrary finite admissibility maps, losses,
and nonadditive maintenance functions, so complexity depends on the input
representation and evaluation oracles. The preceding reductions explain why
an unrestricted tractability claim would be inappropriate. We do not claim
an algorithmic speedup in this paper.

$\{e_{01},e_{34}\}$ is $13.498446$, so the nominal set's exact rational PESS
regret rounds to $0.821788$. The exact archive records four tied PESS/Joint
minimizers at both $B=3$ and $B=5$:
$\{e_{01},e_{34}\}$, $\{e_{01},e_{34},e_{56}\}$, $\{e_{34}\}$, and
$\{e_{34},e_{56}\}$. Nominal has a unique minimizer at $B=3$ and four at
$B=5$. Thus $e_{01}$ has zero incremental PESS-objective benefit when added
to the equally optimal $\{e_{34}\}$ at $B=3$; it is not a uniquely required
part of the pessimistic decision. The table shows the recorded representative
used in the held-out comparison. These exact model values are not comparable
to the PostgreSQL held-out errors below.

The set geometry clarifies this model-level separation. At $B=3$, the
Nominal set $\{e_{12},e_{23},e_{34}\}$ forms a three-edge path and admits two
maximal matchings, $\{e_{12},e_{34}\}$ and $\{e_{23}\}$. Every listed
PESS/Joint optimum is conflict-free, including the equally optimal
$\{e_{34}\}$, and therefore has a singleton maximal-matching family in this
instance. This satisfies the conflict-free certificate, which does not
predict which conflict-free set the optimizer will choose. At $B=5$, the
displayed representatives use three resources for Nominal and two for
PESS/Joint; the exact archive also contains tied supersets, so these
representative cardinalities are not unique optimizer outcomes.

The paired query-level comparison has 34 IDs with higher PESS error, 11 with
lower error, and 15 ties; the mean difference is $+0.02422$ for the displayed
PESS deployment minus the displayed Nominal deployment. This is a descriptive
comparison of different as-built effective sets: the former has only the
$e_{34}$ MCV payload, while the latter has three payloads. The held-out
summary for $\{e_{01},e_{34}\}$ is exactly the same as for $\{e_{34}\}$,
consistent with the missing $e_{01}$ payload and the model's tied optimum.
Consequently, these observations do not identify a causal effect of policy
uncertainty or an intrinsic price of robustness. Figure~\ref{fig:paired}
displays the 60 query-level comparisons; they are not a population ranking,
significance claim, or evidence that PESS generally harms estimates.

For these frozen outputs, the conflict-free condition supplies a direct

\FloatBarrier
\section{Discussion}

The frozen models show that policy information can matter to resource choice,
but not in every instance. Nominal and PESS optima differ at $B=3$ in the
six-task rationalized model, including after restricting to effective-MCV
candidates; they coincide for $B\leq3$ in both larger valid-only models.
Policy sensitivity therefore depends on the prescribed schemes and their
losses. Conflict alone does not establish different losses or optimal sets,
and schemes can differ without edge conflict; the conflict-free result is
only a sufficient certificate.

The 47.49\% Forest10 and 46.27\% Census13 rates count raw candidate graphs
with multiple maximal matchings. Strict syntax screens retain 135 and 58 of
10,000 queries, and the archived intersection is unknown; neither measure
realized PostgreSQL ambiguity. For the six-task held-out comparison, the
reported PESS representative $\{e_{01},e_{34}\}$ had only $e_{34}$ built,
while Nominal had three effective payloads. The PESS representative's error
summary equals that for $\{e_{34}\}$; the $+0.024223$ difference from Nominal
is descriptive over 60 query IDs and two repetitions (120 rows), not causal
policy regret. The larger test comparison has identical Nominal/PESS sets
and no treatment contrast.

PostgreSQL requests, catalog objects, payloads, task eligibility, admissible
schemes, selected schemes, and estimates are distinct. The restricted 16.14
maximal-matching correspondence is conditional on stated source premises and
a two-order fixture; it establishes neither $\mathcal R_i(M)\subseteq
\Gamma_i(M)$ nor a real worst-case guarantee. Taskwise PESS maxima also need
not be realized by one shared policy.

The frozen composition check multiplied singleton corrections for both
created objects. For one disjoint pair, PostgreSQL selected both and the
product matched one observation to floating-point precision. For each
overlapping pair it selected only one object, although the comparison
multiplied both. The residuals were $0.1590$ and $-0.4904$. They reject
that all-created product, not the matching-conditioned product over the
selected matching.
The check does not broadly validate or refute matched-scheme predictions.
Their fidelity remains unknown, making counterfactual complete-scheme loss
prediction the principal scientific gap: can it support externally
meaningful resource decisions under unknown estimator policy?

Positive pre-clamp absolute log error requires positive estimates; finalized
\texttt{Plan Rows}, maintenance cost, and query runtime are different
quantities. Generic $\operatorname{Maint}(M)$ remains nonadditive. The frozen
studies measure neither physical maintenance cost nor latency, and the
Forest10 test split has template overlap and prior exposure. Prior work
narrows the possible ESS distinction to resource advice under a
resource-dependent complete-scheme family and partial selector information,
but the manuscript does not establish sufficient regular-paper novelty.

\section{Conclusion}

ESS separates budgeted resource choice, prescribed scheme admissibility,
policy information, and counterfactual scheme losses. Frozen models show
both policy-sensitive and insensitive decisions. PostgreSQL instantiation
and deployment evidence remain conditional and noncausal; counterfactual
loss fidelity and sufficient technical novelty remain unestablished.
