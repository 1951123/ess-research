\begin{abstract}
Choosing which extended statistics to maintain is a resource allocation
problem, but an installed set need not determine which overlapping
statistics a cardinality estimator applies. An advisor with incomplete
knowledge of the estimator's downstream application strategy may therefore
evaluate the same resource set under different complete estimation schemes.
We formulate extended-statistics selection under estimator-strategy
uncertainty, separating unordered resource decisions, task-specific
admissible scheme families, loss and maintenance constraints. We distinguish
fixed-priority Nominal from task-wise pessimistic (PESS) selection and from
risk induced by one global priority shared across tasks. For a fixed
resource set, expected global-priority loss is at most worst shared-global
loss, which is at most task-wise pessimistic loss under common model and
admissibility assumptions. For a labeled maximal-matching instance, we characterize
joint realization under a shared priority, derive a tight
task-count bound on PESS conservatism, and embed classical
Maximum Acyclic Subgraph hardness. A restricted source-level
correspondence connects this instance to PostgreSQL 16.14
two-column MCV selection.

On four frozen AreCEL workloads, we combine exact offline priority
analysis, a real Train-task scheme-space witness and held-out PostgreSQL
\texttt{Plan Rows} measurements. At budget five under the canonical physical
configuration, Nominal has lower Test P95 Q-error on all four datasets.
Under a separately specified, sampled \emph{counterfactual} uniform global
relative-OID-order distribution, PESS has lower estimated expected
per-order Test P95 on Census13 and DMV11, Nominal does on Forest10, and the
Power7 contrast is inconclusive within the permutation-sampling interval.
Uniform OID ordering is an evaluation assumption, not a production
randomization claim. Our results establish consequential strategy assumptions
in this model and a bounded PostgreSQL manifestation, not universal PESS
superiority or a physical worst-case guarantee.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cardinality estimates shape the costs used by query optimizers and may
influence their plan choices \cite{leis2015optimizers,han2022benchmark}.
Single-column statistics are often insufficient for correlated attributes;
multivariate or extended summaries can improve selectivity estimates
\cite{pg16stats}. Such statistics also incur collection and refresh
overheads. A workload-aware advisor must decide which candidate objects
to maintain under a resource budget, a longstanding statistics-management
problem \cite{chaudhuri2000stats,bruno2002sit,elhelw2009statadvisor}.

Installing a statistic, however, is not equivalent to using it for every
eligible cardinality estimate. Two objects may overlap in the predicates
they cover; an estimator may consume one and thereby make the other
inapplicable to that estimation step. A priority rule or tie resolution
can determine which information is used. If the advisor knows the installed
unordered set but not the downstream rule well enough, the set alone
does not determine a unique estimate. This is \emph{estimator-strategy
uncertainty}: uncertainty about selecting a complete application scheme
from the statistics made available.

Figure~\ref{fig:path} shows a deliberately restricted example. Three
pairwise objects for \(AB\), \(BC\) and \(CD\) are installed, with
overlapping endpoints representing conflicts. If a valid complete scheme
is an inclusion-maximal matching of available edges, the same installed
set admits \(\{s_{AB},s_{CD}\}\) or \(\{s_{BC}\}\). A greedy
priority that considers \(BC\) first chooses the latter; a priority
that considers \(AB\) first chooses the former. Assign hypothetical
task losses \(0.1\) and \(2.0\), respectively. Installing \(BC\) in
addition to \(AB,CD\) preserves the first choice but introduces the
second, increasing the \emph{worst modeled} loss. This example is
mathematical, not a measured PostgreSQL loss.

Strategy uncertainty is distinct from uncertainty in sampled statistics
or underlying data, and also from generalization from training to unseen
queries. It can matter even for fixed data, sample, workload and installed
resources. An implementation may be deterministic but unknown to the
advisor; uncertainty here need not mean runtime randomization.

We model the advisor's decision as an unordered installed set \(M\).
For each cardinality-estimation task \(i\), the selected resources induce
a prescribed family of \emph{complete} admissible schemes
\(\Gamma_i(M)\). A policy determines which member is applied. When that
policy is specified, a Nominal objective is natural. Without dependable
shared-policy information, a task-wise pessimistic objective (PESS) can
hedge against all admissible schemes, while possibly being overly
conservative when a single global policy couples tasks. These are
different decisions under different information assumptions, not merely
competing heuristics for an identical objective.

We pursue three research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} Do controlled changes
in application priority alter applied overlapping statistics and actual
PostgreSQL cardinality estimates? \textbf{RQ2:} How do Nominal and PESS
change frozen resource choices and their model-level priority sensitivity?
\textbf{RQ3:} How do these choices compare in physical held-out error under
an explicitly \emph{counterfactual} uniform global relative-OID ordering?

Our contributions are: (i) an estimator-agnostic interface separating
installed resources, complete admissible schemes and policy knowledge;
(ii) shared-priority realizability, tight conservatism bounds, and a
restricted complexity connection within the pairwise matching model;
(iii) a conditional PostgreSQL 16.14 source-level and traced connection;
and (iv) frozen Train-model and physical Test comparisons, including a
real scheme-space witness and query-level diagnostic attribution. Related research already studies interacting
statistics and alternative complete estimation paths
\cite{bruno2004conditional,chen2022ceg,elhelw2009statadvisor}.
Our narrower focus is \emph{upstream resource selection when the advisor
does not control or fully know the downstream scheme choice}. We claim
neither a novel general robust optimizer nor a universally better PESS
resource set.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Tasks, resources and maintenance}
Let \(\Tasks\) be a finite family of cardinality-estimation tasks.
A task identifies a relational cardinality target and its estimation
context; it need not be an entire SQL query or a physical plan node.
Its true cardinality is \(N_i\geq 0\), positive reference target is
\(T_i=\max\{1,N_i\}\), and workload weight is \(w_i>0\).
Let \(\E\) be a finite universe of estimation resources. The advisor
chooses one \emph{unordered} global set \(M\subseteq\E\), not an
application order or a separate scheme for each task.

Let \(\Maint:2^\E\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\) be additional
maintenance cost relative to the baseline, normalized by
\(\Maint(\varnothing)=0\). It can depend on fixed external data state,
refresh frequency, maintenance horizon and accounting conventions.
The feasible sets at budget \(B\geq0\) are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:feasible}
\mathcal F_B=\{M\subseteq\E:\Maint(M)\leq B\}.
\end{equation}
The generic maintenance function need not be additive, monotone or
submodular. Section~\ref{sec:protocol} introduces the much narrower
per-object \emph{proxy} used for the reported selections.

\subsection{Complete schemes and admissibility}
Each task \(i\) has a finite universe \(\Omega_i\) of \emph{complete}
estimation schemes. A scheme \(\sigma\) specifies enough context to
produce an estimate, possibly including the used statistics, their
predicate coverage and estimator-specific decisions. It need not be a
resource subset, matching or execution plan. Define
\(\operatorname{used}(\sigma)\subseteq\E\), and prescribe
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gamma}
\Gamma_i:2^\E\longrightarrow 2^{\Omega_i}\setminus\{\varnothing\},
\qquad
\sigma\in\Gamma_i(M)\Rightarrow
\operatorname{used}(\sigma)\subseteq M.
\end{equation}
A baseline scheme is available when \(M=\varnothing\). We do not
assume \(\Gamma_i(M_1)\subseteq\Gamma_i(M_2)\) whenever
\(M_1\subseteq M_2\): installing a new object may invalidate
a previously \emph{complete} scheme under the modeled rule.
Nonnested families alone do not prove that every associated
objective is nonmonotone; losses and policy choices also matter.

The distinction between \(M\) and \(\Gamma_i(M)\) separates
the advisor's control from the estimator's modeled options.
The advisor installs one shared resource set, while a member
of \(\Gamma_i(M)\) describes a complete way to produce the
estimate for task \(i\) from resources in that set. Thus the
family records competing complete applications; it is neither
a second decision variable chosen independently by the advisor
nor a claim that every resource in \(M\) is used. Its dependence
on \(M\) matters: changing the installed resources can change
which applications count as complete, even when the old resources
remain installed. For example, in the matching instance the
single edge \(s_{AB}\) has maximal family
\(\{\{s_{AB}\}\}\), while adding a disjoint edge \(s_{CD}\)
leaves only the complete matching \(\{s_{AB},s_{CD}\}\).
The old matching is no longer maximal. This is nonnestedness
of feasible scheme families; whether a scalar loss objective
rises or falls is a separate question determined by the losses
on the available schemes. If both schemes have equal loss,
the taskwise maximum can remain unchanged despite this
nonnestedness; a larger loss on a newly admissible scheme can
instead make a particular objective increase.

Crucially, \(\Gamma_i(M)\) is a \emph{prescribed modeling family}.
If \(\mathcal R_i(M)\) denotes schemes physically realizable by
a deployed estimator, neither \(\mathcal R_i(M)=\Gamma_i(M)\)
nor even \(\mathcal R_i(M)\subseteq\Gamma_i(M)\) follows from
the definitions. Physical worst-case claims would need separate
containment and metric alignment arguments.

\subsection{Estimates, loss and application policies}
For each task, let \(\operatorname{Est}_i:\Omega_i\to
\mathbb R_{>0}\) be a positive pre-output-transform estimate and
\(\ell_i:\Omega_i\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\) a loss function.
The illustrative absolute logarithmic loss is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
L_i(\sigma)=w_i\ell_i(\sigma)
=w_i\left|\log\frac{\operatorname{Est}_i(\sigma)}{T_i}\right|.
\end{equation}
This is not the same observable as rounded or clamped PostgreSQL
\texttt{Plan Rows} Q-error.

A nonempty finite family \(\Theta\) of application-policy parameters
may induce a complete scheme \(\sigma_i(M;\theta)\) for each task,
installed set and policy. When the model and policy family are
compatible, \(\sigma_i(M;\theta)\in\Gamma_i(M)\) for all
relevant \(i,M,\theta\). One \(\theta\) shared among tasks couples their
scheme choices; it need not be probabilistic.

\subsection{Pairwise MCV admissibility and loss}
For the structured instance, each candidate \(e\in\E\) is a
\emph{labeled} two-attribute MCV object, with endpoints
\(A_e\), \(|A_e|=2\). For each task \(i\), statically
eligible objects \(\E_i\subseteq\E\) form the edge-labeled
multigraph \(H_i=(V_i,\E_i)\). The installed eligible graph
\(H_i[M]\) contains the edges \(M\cap\E_i\). Distinct
statistics with identical attribute pairs remain distinct edges.
A matching consists of pairwise endpoint-disjoint edges.
We prescribe
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mm}
\Gamma_i^{\mathrm{MM}}(M)=\MM(H_i[M]).
\end{equation}
\emph{Maximal} means no additional available edge can be appended;
it does not mean maximum cardinality. Even an empty graph has
the empty matching as its unique maximal matching.

For a positive baseline pre-clamp estimate \(n_i^0>0\) and
fixed task-specific correction factors \(r_{e,i}>0\), the controlled
multiplicative approximation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:multiplicative}
\widehat n_i(P)=n_i^0\prod_{e\in P}r_{e,i},
\quad
L_i(P)=w_i\Bigl|\alpha_i+\sum_{e\in P}b_{e,i}\Bigr|,
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha_i=\log(n_i^0/T_i)\) and
\(b_{e,i}=\log r_{e,i}\).
Positivity is necessary before taking logarithms.
This separability is \emph{assumed for the model}, not a theorem
about physical PostgreSQL MCV estimation or independence of
underlying attributes. In the actual frozen study, the
\texttt{census13-clamped-log-v2} and related dataset-specific
coefficient constructions follow pinned normalization and
quantization rules, with exact \texttt{Fraction} reference loss.
The physical \texttt{Plan Rows} metric is evaluated separately.

\section{Optimization Objectives and Relationships}
\label{sec:objectives}

\subsection{Strategy knowledge defines the problem}
For a fixed \(M\in\mathcal F_B\) and compatible nonempty finite
policy family \(\Theta\), the following are distinct objectives:
\begin{align}
F_{\Nom}(M;\theta_0)
 &=\sum_{i\in\Tasks}L_i(\sigma_i(M;\theta_0)),\label{eq:nom}\\
F_{\mathrm{joint}}(M)
 &=\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{i\in\Tasks}
 L_i(\sigma_i(M;\theta)),\label{eq:joint}\\
F_{\PESS}(M)
 &=\sum_{i\in\Tasks}\max_{\sigma\in\Gamma_i(M)}
 L_i(\sigma).\label{eq:pess}
\end{align}
The advisor's optimization is \(\min_{M\in\mathcal F_B}F(M)\)
for its chosen information assumption. The advisor does not
simultaneously choose the worst-case scheme or its application
policy. For the \emph{same fixed} \(M\), compatibility and common
loss imply
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:genericbound}
F_{\Nom}(M;\theta_0)\leq
F_{\mathrm{joint}}(M)\leq F_{\PESS}(M).
\end{equation}
The second inequality may be strict because separate taskwise
worst schemes may not be jointly attainable.

These objectives encode information available to the advisor,
not three implementation choices for one known system. If a
downstream deterministic policy is known, Nominal evaluates
its selected scheme. If the policy is deterministic but unknown,
there is no probability distribution to average: \(G\) asks for
the worst aggregate loss over the modeled shared policies.
The mean \(A\) answers a different, explicitly hypothetical
stochastic question; its uniform distribution over global orders
is an assumption on the evaluation, not an assertion that the
system randomizes. PESS removes the common-policy constraint
altogether and lets each task attain its own admissible maximum.
For a fixed \(M\), these information assumptions can therefore
give different evaluations without changing the installed set.

\subsection{One shared global priority}
For the pairwise instance, let \(\Pi(M)\) contain all strict
permutations of the \emph{labeled selected resources}, including
irrelevant edges. The same order \(\pi\) applies to every task,
restricted to that task's eligible installed edges. A greedy scan
accepts an edge if both endpoints are unused and produces
\(\gamma_i(M,\pi)\in\Gamma_i^{\mathrm{MM}}(M)\).
Let \(Z(M,\pi)=\sum_iL_i(\gamma_i(M,\pi))\) and define
\begin{align}
N_{\pi_0}(M)&=Z(M,\pi_0),&
A(M)&=\frac{\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(M)}Z(M,\pi)}{|\Pi(M)|},
\label{eq:na}\\
G(M)&=\max_{\pi\in\Pi(M)}Z(M,\pi),&
P(M)&=\sum_i\max_{Q\in\Gamma_i^{\mathrm{MM}}(M)}L_i(Q).
\label{eq:gp}
\end{align}
Here \(\pi_0\) is the restriction of one fixed total order on
the candidate universe, implemented as candidate-ID order
in the frozen study. \(A\) is \emph{uniform over global
permutations}, not over distinct resulting matchings.
\(G\) is the largest total loss under \emph{one common} order;
\(P\) chooses each task's largest admissible loss independently.
The same order in \(G\) couples task outcomes even when their
eligible graphs differ: improving one task's matching can
preclude a different matching preferred by another task.
By contrast, the summation in \(P\) takes a separate maximum
before adding losses, so its maximizing tuple need not be the
output of any common order.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-set global-order bounds]
\label{prop:bounds}
For every fixed \(M\) in the prescribed pairwise model and the same
finite task losses,
\[
A(M)\leq G(M)\leq P(M),\qquad N_{\pi_0}(M)\leq G(M).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The mean and any particular member of the finite
collection \(\{Z(M,\pi)\}\) do not exceed its maximum.
For any \(\pi\) and task \(i\), greedy produces an admissible
maximal matching, whose loss is at most that task's maximal
admissible loss. Summing and maximizing over \(\pi\) proves
\(G(M)\leq P(M)\).
\end{proof}
These inequalities compare \emph{modeled loss on the same fixed set}.
Neither \(A(M)\) nor \(N_{\pi_0}(M)\) universally dominates
the other. They do not rank separately selected resource sets or
bound physical PostgreSQL Q-error without additional assumptions.

\begin{proposition}[Single-task order completeness]
\label{prop:completion}
For a fixed task graph \(H_i[M]\), every inclusion-maximal
matching is produced by greedy under some freely chosen
abstract total edge priority.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put the matching's edges first. Since those edges are disjoint,
greedy accepts them all. By inclusion-maximality every remaining
edge meets an accepted edge, so none is subsequently accepted.
\end{proof}
This proves neither cross-task simultaneous completeness nor
physical realizability of every abstract ordering within
a fixed PostgreSQL catalog.

\subsection{A strict coupling gap and nonmonotone example}
On the path in Figure~\ref{fig:path}, four of the six global
permutations select \(\{s_{AB},s_{CD}\}\), whereas two select
\(\{s_{BC}\}\). Uniform orders therefore weight the two
matchings \(2/3\) and \(1/3\), not \(1/2\) each.
For two tasks with the \emph{same} path and losses
\(L_1(Q_1)=1,L_1(Q_2)=0\) but
\(L_2(Q_1)=0,L_2(Q_2)=1\), every shared priority yields
total loss one; the independent taskwise maxima sum to two:
\(A(M)=G(M)=1<P(M)=2\). This is an abstract witness to
conservatism in \(P\), not a dataset measurement.

Similarly, take the installed set \(\{s_{AB},s_{CD}\}\)
with sole complete matching \(Q_1\) of loss \(0.1\).
Adding the conflicting resource \(s_{BC}\) permits \(Q_2\)
of loss \(2.0\), so this particular PESS objective increases
from \(0.1\) to \(2.0\). Nonmonotonicity is thus possible,
rather than a general law that new statistics harm estimates.

\subsection{Shared-priority realizability and complexity}
\label{sec:shared-theory}
The coupling in \(G\) admits a constructive characterization.
Fix \(M\) and one inclusion-maximal matching \(S_i\) in each
task graph \(H_i[M]\). A common strict edge order realizes
all these prescribed matchings if and only if, for every
eligible \(e\notin S_i\), at least one target edge
\(f\in S_i\) that shares an endpoint with \(e\) precedes
\(e\). Necessity follows because a rejected edge must
have an earlier accepted blocker; sufficiency follows
by considering the first departure from any target matching.

This condition yields a polynomial-time feasibility test
\emph{when the targets are supplied}. Starting from an empty
order, append any remaining edge that, in every task,
is either ineligible, a target edge, or already blocked
by an appended target edge. If no such edge exists,
the targets are incompatible. To see why arbitrary safe
choices work, move a safe edge to the front of any
feasible remaining suffix: a target cannot block another
target, and a non-target is already blocked where eligible.
The existence of a feasible completion is preserved.
This does not solve the search over unspecified taskwise
worst matchings or the computation of \(G(M)\).

\begin{proposition}[Tight task-count conservatism]
\label{prop:taskfactor}
For a fixed installed set \(M\), \(t\geq1\) tasks,
nonnegative modeled losses, and freely ordered
labeled maximal-matchings,
\[
G(M)\leq P(M)\leq tG(M).
\]
The factor \(t\) is tight.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For task \(i\), let \(p_i\) be its largest
admissible loss. A priority that begins with
a maximal matching attaining \(p_i\) realizes
that loss. Since other task losses are
nonnegative, \(G(M)\geq p_i\) for every \(i\).
Thus \(P(M)=\sum_i p_i\leq tG(M)\).

For tightness, let all \(t\) tasks share a
\(t\)-edge star. Task \(i\) has loss one only
when \(e_i\) is selected. Each task has
worst loss one, but a common order selects
the same first star edge in every task,
so \(P=t\) and \(G=1\).
\end{proof}

Shared ordering can also be computationally difficult
despite trivial task-level choices. Given a loopless
directed graph \(D=(V,A_D)\), install one labeled star
edge \(e_v\) per vertex. For every arc \(u\to v\),
make a task eligible for only the conflicting
edges \(e_u,e_v\), with normalized modeled loss
one if \(e_u\) is chosen and zero if \(e_v\) is chosen.
The loss summed over tasks under a common order is
exactly the number of arcs oriented forward by that
order. Consequently, computing \(G(M)\) is NP-hard
even with two eligible edges per task, by the classical
Maximum Acyclic Subgraph / Feedback Arc Set problem
\cite{karp1972reducibility}. This is a result for the
\emph{fixed-set abstract model}, not a hardness claim
about outer resource selection or PostgreSQL itself.
For the original natural-log loss, positive rational
corrections \(2\) and \(1\) give scores \(\ln 2\)
and zero; the exact integer threshold problem is
stated after normalization by \(\ln 2\).
The restricted construction does not establish the
complexity of deciding \(P(M)=G(M)\) in general.
Full proofs, encoding details, and synthetic
verification are archived in the independently
reviewed Issue~\#123 theory note.

\subsection{Frozen resource-selection procedure}
The empirical selection trains \emph{only} \(N_{\pi_0}\)
and \(P\), using the same strictly improving, deterministic
add-only greedy heuristic, separately for each Train workload
and budget. Its feasibility is the experimental
\(\sum_{e\in M}\bar m_e\leq B\), with nonnegative
per-object \emph{proxy} costs. The heuristic adds the feasible
single resource giving the greatest strictly positive loss
decrease, breaking ties by candidate ID, and stops if no
such improvement exists. It performs no swaps or deletions
and does not certify global optimality. The read-only
offline \(A,G\) analysis is \emph{not} a retraining or
a different selection algorithm.

\section{A Scoped PostgreSQL MCV Mechanism}
\label{sec:pg}

\subsection{Version-pinned selector and its prerequisites}
PostgreSQL 16.14's single-relation selectivity path can apply
multivariate MCV objects to still-unestimated compatible
clauses. Source inspection covers four selector functions:\\
{\raggedright
\texttt{clauselist\_selectivity\_ext},\\
\texttt{statext\_mcv\_clauselist\_selectivity},\\
\texttt{choose\_best\_statistics}, and\\
\texttt{RelationGetStatExtList}.\par}
The selector repeats candidate selection over a source-level
OID-sorted candidate list. Under the conditions below,
all surviving eligible two-key objects tie in the ranking,
so list/OID order resolves which object is used first
\cite{pg16stats}. This is a deterministic mechanism
for a fixed catalog, not a source of random orders.
The version-pinned code locations and conditional argument
are recorded in the accompanying project
\href{https://github.com/1951123/ess-research/blob/main/notes/pg-mcv-maximal-matching-correspondence.md}
{source-correspondence note}.

The correspondence assumes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{A1.}] Each relevant clause is a supported simple MCV
  predicate on exactly one distinct base-table attribute. It is
  non-pseudoconstant, with one relevant clause per attribute and no OR,
  expression or repeated-attribute predicates.
\item[\textbf{A2.}] Eligible objects are \emph{built}
  two-attribute, no-expression MCV statistics on the same
  base relation, covering two currently unestimated
  compatible attribute clauses.
\item[\textbf{A3.}] Privilege, relation and inheritance checks
  permit the objects, no other estimator preconsumes those
  clauses, and the candidate list is exactly the
  installed eligible set.
\item[\textbf{A4.}] Every object whose two endpoints remain
  free ties under the selector's key/coverage ranking;
  the fixed list order breaks the tie.
\end{enumerate}

An edge is labeled by the particular built MCV object, not
just its attribute pair: distinct objects on the same pair
can have different contents. Under A1--A3, applying an edge
uses the compatible clauses for both endpoint attributes.
Those clauses are then marked estimated, so an edge sharing
either endpoint cannot also be applied to the remaining
clauses in that estimation step. This is the graph conflict
represented by a shared endpoint. A1--A4 make the available
unconsumed edges exactly the candidates inspected by the
repeated selector, with tied candidates resolved by its fixed
list order. At termination, an unselected edge disjoint from
all selected edges would still cover two free clauses and
would remain eligible, contradicting termination. Hence the
selected objects form an inclusion-wise maximal matching.
For the path in Figure~\ref{fig:path}, one such matching has
two edges and another has one: maximality means no edge can
be appended, not that the matching has maximum cardinality.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional source correspondence]
\label{prop:pg}
Under A1--A4, the selected labeled two-column MCV
objects constitute an inclusion-maximal matching of \(H_i[M]\).
If the abstract tie priority is freely varied over all strict
total orders, its attainable \emph{single-task} outputs
are exactly \(\Gamma_i^{\mathrm{MM}}(M)\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
After selecting a two-attribute object, the corresponding
clauses are marked estimated; subsequent selected objects
cannot share either endpoint. If an unselected edge were
disjoint from every selected edge at termination, its two
clauses would remain eligible, contradicting the terminating
failure to find a candidate. The output is maximal.
Conversely, Proposition~\ref{prop:completion}
orders any chosen maximal matching first.
\end{proof}
Physical priorities are fixed by a catalog state, and
arbitrary global orders need not be achievable without other
changes. This proposition does not cover all PostgreSQL SQL
forms, statistic kinds, estimator stages or output formulas.
In particular, the source argument is restricted to the
single-relation MCV path and clause pattern in A1--A4; joins,
expressions, OR predicates, other extended-statistics kinds,
additional clause consumers and non-tied rankings may follow
different control flow. The correspondence is a useful
structural instance for the abstract model, not a claim that
PostgreSQL implements ESS or that every abstract matching is
realizable in an unchanged physical catalog.

\subsection{Physical mechanism evidence}
A controlled four-attribute trace with objects \(AB,BC,CD\)
used an instrumented PostgreSQL development build. Creating
objects in order \(AB,BC,CD\) produced the selected set
\(\{AB,CD\}\), while \(BC,AB,CD\) produced
\(\{BC\}\). Both terminated without an eligible candidate
and involved built MCV data. This was a scoped fixture-level
observation; recreating objects can otherwise affect sample
contents.

In a later, content-controlled held-out order intervention,
four frozen Nominal \(B=5\) sets were recreated in canonical
or exact-reverse order. Checks fixed the data, replayed
ANALYZE sample, settings and MCV-content fingerprints.
All 80,000 Test EXPLAIN outcomes and MCV diagnostics
completed. Section~\ref{sec:results} quantifies changed
applied-object sequences and actual row-estimation effects.
Neither intervention makes all maximal matchings physically
attainable in every context, or equates the multiplicative
model with PostgreSQL's entire cardinality calculation.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\subsection{Evidence layers and frozen Train selection}
Our evaluation separates resource decisions, exact model-internal priority
analysis, and physical held-out observations. We use Forest10, Census13,
DMV11, and Power7 from the original AreCEL workloads \cite{wang2021arecel}.
Each has 100,000 original Train tasks used for model construction and
selection and 10,000 official Test queries for subsequent PostgreSQL
evaluation. Test labels and estimates never reselect statistics.

These frozen results form five evidence layers, each answering a
different question. First, Train-only \#99 chooses resource sets
using the modeled loss and a per-resource maintenance-cost proxy.
Second, offline \#103 enumerates global priorities exactly on those
already chosen sets to compare model objectives; it executes no
PostgreSQL Test queries. Third, \#116 opens the complete scheme
family for one deterministically chosen Census13 Train task and
counts ambiguous and positive-risk tasks in that frozen model.
Fourth, physical \#101 and \#105 ask what happens under a fixed
canonical catalog order and a controlled canonical/reverse-order
intervention. Fifth, \#107 estimates an expectation over a
hypothetical distribution of global relative-OID orders by sampling
physical Test configurations. Only the last layer averages across
orders, and its uncertainty concerns that permutation sample rather
than new queries, workloads or statistics samples.

The first stage (\#99) selects Nominal and PESS resources on Train with
the same deterministic, strictly improving add-only greedy heuristic
at \(B\in\{0,2,3,4,5\}\). Each candidate's budget cost is one
\emph{proxy unit}, not measured maintenance CPU or storage. All 40
labeled decisions completed, giving 25 unique dataset-specific
physical configurations. The frozen resource manifest is stored at
\href{https://github.com/1951123/ess-research/blob/main/experiments/add-only-greedy-issue99/frozen-train-resources-issue96-v1.json}{the \#99 selection record}.
The frozen Train-model coefficients use positive clamped-log-derived
quantities and exact \texttt{Fraction} reference evaluation; they
are not physical \texttt{Plan Rows} measurements.

A read-only (\#116) Census13 Train study enumerates \(\Gamma_i(M)\)
for five existing B3/B4/B5 selected sets. It deterministically
takes the smallest stable Train task ID satisfying structural,
positive-risk and compact-family conditions. Exact task losses
were checked against a pinned advisor reference, and the
100,000-task objective sums agreed with the existing \#103 model
summary. This is artifact agreement, not independent recollection
of raw Train data or a new resource-selection experiment.

\subsection{Physical PostgreSQL evaluation}
The \#101, \#105 and \#107 physical studies use version-pinned
PostgreSQL 16.14 with full source relations, fixed \#99 ANALYZE
sample replay and effective statistics target 300 for ordinary
and selected extended statistics. The \#105/\#107 build is pinned
to source SHA \texttt{f1bf37b5dd3d3a7087fc0af5de550ca31035854c}.
Settings include these pinned values:\\
{\raggedright
\texttt{plan\_cache\_mode=force\_custom\_plan};\\
\texttt{max\_parallel\_workers\_per\_gather=0};\\
\texttt{client\_min\_messages=warning}.\par}
For controlled order changes,
the protocol verifies actual OID priority, full-table/sample identities,
ordinary statistics and individual MCV-content fingerprints.
Per-query extended-statistics diagnostics are used to verify applied
object and MCV-estimate sequences; EXPLAIN alone is insufficient.

For each original Test query, let \(t\geq0\) denote its truth
and \(e\geq0\) its rounded top-level \texttt{Plan Rows} estimate.
Using the frozen \(r=1\) rounding convention, the zero-aware
AreCEL Q-error is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qerror}
Q(t,e)=
\begin{cases}
1,&t=e=0,\\
t,&t>0,\ e=0,\\
e,&t=0,\ e>0,\\
\max(e/t,t/e),&t>0,\ e>0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The percentile implementation follows the original NumPy convention,
and differs from the selection model's absolute-log loss.
The fixed-order \#101 evaluation completed 25 configurations and
250,000 Test EXPLAINs. The paired canonical/reverse \#105 study
completed eight configurations, 80,000 Test EXPLAINs and complete
selected-object/estimate diagnostics. Both studies use frozen,
previously selected resources.

\subsection{Offline Train-model global order enumeration}
The \#103 study evaluates every strict global priority permutation
on the \emph{already selected} Train-model resource sets:
906 permutations in total for 25 distinct fixed sets. A global
order is shared across tasks and counted even when distinct
orders induce the same matching outcomes. It computes
\(N_{\pi_0}(M),A(M),G(M),P(M)\) using frozen
Train coefficients and exact reference checks, with
\emph{no} Test execution, refitting or reselection.
This evidence is recorded in
\href{https://github.com/1951123/ess-research/pull/104}
{Draft PR \#104}; it should not be mislabeled as an
already merged \texttt{main}-branch experiment artifact.

\subsection{Counterfactual uniform global relative-OID order}
For RQ3, define a \emph{hypothetical} uniform population of
the \(k!\) strict relative-OID orders of each fixed selected set
\(M\). One physical order is held unchanged for all
10,000 official Test queries in that arm. The primary estimand is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:estimand}
Y_\pi(M)=\operatorname{P95}_{q\in\mathrm{Test}}Q(q,M,\pi),
\qquad
\mu(M)=\mathbb E_{\pi\sim U(\Pi(M))}[Y_\pi(M)].
\end{equation}
It is the \emph{expected per-order Test P95}, not the P95
of order-averaged per-query Q-errors. Neither this assumption
nor the abstract global order distribution implies PostgreSQL
assigns OIDs randomly in production.

The frozen \#107 study used \textbf{156 physical configurations},
not a complete 732-arm census. For each of four five-object
Nominal sets it included two canonical/reverse certainty orders
plus 22 simple-random-without-replacement (SRSWOR) orders
from the remaining 118. The two five-object PESS sets each
contributed 24 SRSWOR orders of 120. Two three-object PESS
sets contributed all six orders each. All 156 arms and
\textbf{1,560,000 cumulative Test EXPLAINs} eventually completed.

For Nominal let \(F\) be the mean of its two certainty outcomes,
\(\overline Y_R\) the mean of 22 residual sampled outcomes,
and \(s_R^2\) the usual sample variance. The design-weighted
estimate and variance are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:design}
\widehat\mu_N=\frac{2}{120}F+\frac{118}{120}\overline Y_R,
\quad
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\widehat\mu_N)=
\left(\frac{118}{120}\right)^2
\left(1-\frac{22}{118}\right)\frac{s_R^2}{22}.
\end{equation}
For five-object PESS, \(\widehat\mu_P=\overline Y_{24}\)
and the design variance estimate is \((1-24/120)s_P^2/24\).
The two three-object PESS means are exact six-order
population means conditional on the fixed Test split.
For the independently sampled PESS-minus-Nominal contrast,
the component variance estimates add. The frozen approximate
95\% intervals use \(t_{0.975,21}\) times the design standard
error. They cover \emph{permutation sampling only}, not new
workloads, datasets or ANALYZE samples. A degenerate interval
from 24 sampled orders cannot certify all 120 orders identical.

The \#107 design was specified after inspection of earlier
\#101/\allowbreak\#105 results, although new sampled orders were fixed
before new Test measurements. Eight original \#105 certainty
arms were remeasured after a reuse-eligibility failure whose
exact row-level cause was not preserved. Twelve three-object
PESS arms stopped before EXPLAIN because a validator expected
five objects; an authorized continuation completed those
\emph{same} frozen orders without replacements or extra arms.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}
We distinguish the complete-scheme mechanism under frozen
Train-model loss from observed physical Test Q-error.
The former evaluates prescribed schemes and losses on the
original Train tasks; the latter measures rounded PostgreSQL
\texttt{Plan Rows} on the held-out Test queries. Their units
and policy assumptions differ, so results below are interpreted
within their evidence layer before being compared.

\paragraph{Query-level diagnostics under frozen orders.}
A read-only analysis (\#125) validated the \#107 paired
Test-query records and reproduced official aggregate results.

coincided with an MCV candidate-ID sequence change, but not
vice versa. Relative to the declared anchors, Census13 and
DMV11 Nominal had 17,731 and 37,843 query--order pairs with
both changes. Their PESS counterparts had 87,921 and 89,890
sequence changes with \emph{unchanged} Q-error; aligned numeric

population estimates or causal tests. The \#105 fixed-order
pairs were not recounted as new \#125 comparisons.

invariance of its 96 unsampled orders. Forest10

\emph{total Train-model absolute-log loss} over
abstract priority permutations; \#107 estimates

Q-error} over sampled relative-OID permutations.
They differ in split, loss, aggregation and
estimation mechanism. Agreement or disagreement
between rankings is not, by itself, evidence of
model calibration or out-of-sample generalization.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:validity}
\paragraph{Prescribed model and physical coverage.}
The family \(\Gamma_i(M)\) is not known generally to
contain all physically realizable \(\mathcal R_i(M)\).
Even if it did, a physical-error upper bound would
also require agreement of the estimator and loss
definitions. The maximal-matching source
correspondence requires A1--A4; other predicate
forms, joins, competing statistic kinds and
estimation stages can invalidate it.
Adding an edge can make an old maximal matching
non-maximal, but \emph{nonnested schemes alone}
do not establish nonmonotonicity of arbitrary
fixed-policy or expected-loss objectives.
The Census13 witness was deterministically
chosen to exhibit a mechanism, not sampled to
represent all tasks, and does not demonstrate
general family nonnestedness.

\paragraph{Resource and metric proxies.}
The training selections use add-only greedy,
not a globally optimal outer solver.
Their per-statistic maintenance budget is a
proxy, not measured refresh CPU, I/O,
update overhead or execution time. The modeled
absolute-log task losses are not actual
PostgreSQL Plan Rows Q-error, nor are lower
Q-errors equivalent to improved plan latency.
A stronger claim would need direct physical
maintenance and end-to-end plan measurements.

\paragraph{Order assumptions and inference.}
A real catalog has a fixed OID order; unknown
strategy does not imply stochastic behavior.
Uniform abstract priorities and uniform
relative-OID physical test orders are
\emph{counterfactual}, not learned production
distributions. The sampled 156-arm physical
experiment does not enumerate every five-object
order. Its approximate intervals quantify
permutation-sampling uncertainty conditional
on the fixed Test split. Degenerate sample
variance among 24 of 120 orders is not
population certainty. Order sampling was
designed after seeing earlier results;
execution exceptions and the unknown
row-level cause of one reuse check are
retained in the study record.

\paragraph{Generalization and reproducibility.}
The four original AreCEL splits, two-column
single-relation MCV instance and pinned
instrumented PostgreSQL 16.14 build do not
cover arbitrary workloads, DBMS versions,
estimators or statistics kinds. The study
uses a target of 300 and does not reproduce
the full published AreCEL learned-estimator
environment \cite{wang2021arecel}.
Frozen protocols, findings, code and hashes
are versioned in the repository, while
irreplaceable query-level raw physical
observations and manifests remain in
documented external runtime archives.
The evidence cannot establish universal
PESS superiority, a real-world OID
distribution or unconditional physical
worst-case bounds.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Statistics management and advisors.}
Selecting optimizer statistics under a
space or maintenance budget is established
work. Chaudhuri and Narasayya search for
essential statistics sets that preserve a
chosen optimizer plan/cost criterion; their
method considers multicolumn statistics,
allows multiple essential sets, and reports
that shrinking order can affect the set
found \cite{chaudhuri2000stats}. This is
already evidence that resource choices can
interact with optimizer behavior, rather
than an independent-value knapsack model.
Their target is an equivalent essential
subset for a specified optimizer criterion;
we instead evaluate a set against complete
admissible applications when the advisor
lacks a dependable policy for which one is
used.

Bruno and Chaudhuri's work on statistics
for intermediate query expressions (SITs)
is a direct statistics-advisor precedent
that already treats uncertainty and
sensitivity \cite{bruno2002sit}. It uses
Min/Max hypothetical value distributions
to propagate extreme cardinality and
selectivity estimates, then assesses the
resulting plan-cost spread and candidate
SIT contributions. We therefore do not
claim that uncertainty-aware statistics
recommendations originate here. The
uncertainty considered there concerns
attribute distributions, selectivity and
optimizer cost response; ESS models
uncertainty about which complete estimator
application is selected from the family
induced by installed resources. SITs are
statistics on intermediate expressions,
whereas our physical instance is restricted
to single-table two-column MCV objects.

StatAdvisor explicitly models dependencies
among statistical views and groups views
whose joint plan benefit is not captured by
their isolated benefits \cite{elhelw2009statadvisor}.
CORDS discovers correlated column pairs
for joint-statistics recommendations
\cite{ilyas2004cords}; query-feedback work
detects and ranks attribute dependencies,
including under a system-catalog space
budget \cite{haas2007dependencies}. These
works rule out novelty claims for
interaction-aware advice, multivariate
statistics recommendation or constrained
resource selection. Their resource and
benefit models differ from the explicit
family of complete task-level applications
and policy-information-dependent risks
studied here. Broader physical-design
relaxations address constrained choices
such as indexes and materialized views
\cite{bruno2005relaxation}; they are useful
context, but not direct evidence about
uncertain selection among installed
statistics schemes.

\paragraph{Alternative complete estimation paths.}
Bruno and Chaudhuri's conditional selectivity
approach already considers conflicting SITs
and alternative estimator-side decompositions
\cite{bruno2004conditional}. Cardinality
Estimation Graphs explicitly represent
alternative complete summary-based paths
and analyze fixed or undefined path-selection
rules \cite{chen2022ceg}. These are close
antecedents for multiple applicable schemes
and ambiguous estimator-side choices.
In particular, conditional-selectivity
identities and dynamic programming are used
to compare alternative ways to combine
available SITs for an estimate. CEGs make
alternative complete formulas explicit as
paths through subqueries and study path
selection strategies. We therefore do not
claim that competing complete estimators,
decompositions or uncertainty about a path
choice are new. Those papers primarily ask
how an estimator can construct or select an
accurate application path. Our decision
object is upstream: which statistics to
install under a budget when that installation
induces a resource-dependent family of
complete schemes and the advisor does not
know the later choice rule. A CEG path or a
SIT decomposition is not asserted to be a
matching or to correspond to PostgreSQL's
MCV selector.
Our narrower distinction is \emph{upstream
budgeted statistics installation} \(M\)
whose \emph{resource-dependent} complete
admissible family \(\Gamma_i(M)\) is evaluated
by an advisor that lacks a dependable
downstream scheme-selection policy.
Neither CEG paths nor SIT decompositions
are claimed to be PostgreSQL matchings.

\paragraph{Ordering and complexity precedents.}
The NP-hardness ingredient in Section~\ref{sec:shared-theory}
is the classical Feedback Arc Set problem
\cite{karp1972reducibility}, not a new hardness result
for ordering. Minimum maximal matching is also
classically hard \cite{yannakakis1980edge}, and work
on weighted greedy matching studies different
priority constraints \cite{deligkas2017weighted}.
Our limited contribution is to identify how a
\emph{single shared resource-edge order} couples
multiple simple estimator tasks in the prescribed
ESS model. General decision-dependent uncertainty
is established robust-optimization territory
\cite{nohadani2018decision}; neither that idea
nor generic precedence feasibility is claimed
as novel. A complete originality claim for the
particular common-edge-order feasibility test
would require further prior-art verification.

\paragraph{Estimation quality and robust decisions.}
PostgreSQL supports several extended
statistics kinds, including MCV lists
\cite{pg16stats}. LpBound uses pessimistic
cardinality \emph{upper bounds} from degree
statistics \cite{zhang2025lpbound};
our PESS criterion instead maximizes
\emph{modeled loss across application schemes},
not cardinality itself. PARQO and robust
plan coresets make robust \emph{plan-choice}
decisions under uncertain estimates
\cite{xiu2024parqo,raychaudhury2026coresets},
rather than install statistics under
uncertain downstream application policies.
These methods share a concern with uncertainty
but use different uncertainty sets and
decision objects: LpBound bounds a
cardinality quantity, while PARQO and
coresets choose robust plans or plan families.
None of those statements implies a bound
from the ESS model loss to physical Q-error,
execution latency or plan quality.
Cardinality-estimation benchmarks caution
that Q-error and final plan quality are
distinct \cite{leis2015optimizers,han2022benchmark}.
The original AreCEL work supplies the workload
family \cite{wang2021arecel}, not the
estimator we execute.

These precedents establish interactive
statistics, alternative complete estimation
methods and robust database decisions as
existing topics. Our claimed contribution
is the specific \emph{advisor--estimator
interface}, not first recognition of
scheme alternatives or a general theorem
of robust optimization. The comparison
is focused rather than an exhaustive
negative literature search.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
An advisor controls \emph{which statistics to install};
the estimator controls \emph{which available statistics
are applied}. ESS makes this information gap explicit
through resource-dependent complete scheme families and
objectives corresponding to different policy knowledge.
For one fixed resource set and aligned modeled loss,
\(A(M)\leq G(M)\leq P(M)\), and the taskwise PESS envelope
can be a tight factor of the task count above shared-order
risk. Shared-order feasibility is constructive when
target matchings are specified, while optimizing a common
order inherits classical hardness in a restricted model.
These are abstract, fixed-set results, not physical
Q-error guarantees.

The frozen Census13 Train witness shows that complete
schemes for the same task can have different modeled
losses. Conditional PostgreSQL 16.14 source reasoning and
controlled Test interventions exhibit physical order
sensitivity. At canonical order, B5 Nominal has lower
Test P95 in all four workloads; for the separate
counterfactual mean \emph{per-order} P95, PESS is favored
on Census13 and DMV11, Nominal on Forest10, and Power7
is inconclusive within its sampling interval.
Query-level diagnostics further show that changed MCV
application sequences need not change Q-error.
The two evaluations answer different policy questions;
neither establishes universal PESS superiority or
causal mediation by an individual MCV event.

The practical implication is to \emph{state the assumed
application policy} when recommending a resource set:
known deterministic priority, a declared distribution
over shared priorities, worst shared priority, or
independent taskwise worst schemes. PESS can be a
conservative modeled envelope, but its taskwise
maximizers need not form one joint execution.
The present evaluation uses maintenance-cost proxies,
frozen workloads and a limited pairwise MCV setting;
physical policy coverage and loss alignment remain
unverified in general. Future work includes grounded
policy families, stronger outer selection methods,
measured maintenance costs and end-to-end plan effects.
