\begin{abstract}
An advisor chooses which extended statistics to maintain, but
the installed set need not determine which \emph{complete estimation
scheme} a cardinality estimator eventually applies. We formulate
Extended Statistics Selection (ESS) under \emph{estimation-scheme
uncertainty}, encompassing incomplete knowledge of deterministic
application policies and intrinsic selection randomness. The
advisor controls an unordered resource set; admissible schemes,
their joint cross-task uncertainty, workload aggregation, and risk
evaluation are modeled separately. Fixed-reference Nominal,
worst shared-policy, and task-wise pessimistic (PESS) objectives
are instances of this formulation. We distinguish finite-sample
optimization from population risk and establish conditional workload-risk consistency results
for finite shared-policy classes and task-wise pessimism.

We instantiate ESS with restricted PostgreSQL 16.14 two-column
MCV selection. Under explicit conditions, source-level selection
corresponds to maximal matching. For this structured instance,
we characterize joint realizability under one priority, prove a
tight task-count bound on PESS conservatism, and relate fixed-set
shared-order optimization to Maximum Acyclic Subgraph hardness.
Its multiplicative scheme-loss model is an approximation, not
a reproduction of physical PostgreSQL estimation.

On four frozen AreCEL workloads, we compare modeled Train
selection with held-out PostgreSQL \texttt{Plan Rows} evidence.
At budget five, Nominal has lower canonical-order Test P95
Q-error on all four datasets. Under a separately specified,
sampled \emph{counterfactual} uniform relative-OID-order
distribution, PESS has lower estimated expected per-order Test
P95 on Census13 and DMV11; Nominal does on Forest10, while
Power7 remains inconclusive within the sampling interval.
These results show meaningful strategy sensitivity without
claiming that PESS universally outperforms Nominal or that
modeled bounds transfer to physical Q-error.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cardinality estimates shape the costs used by query optimizers and may
influence their plan choices \cite{leis2015optimizers,han2022benchmark}.
Single-column statistics are often insufficient for correlated attributes;
multivariate or extended summaries can improve selectivity estimates
\cite{pg16stats}. Such statistics also incur collection and refresh
overheads. A workload-aware advisor must decide which candidate objects
to maintain under a resource budget, a longstanding statistics-management
problem \cite{chaudhuri2000stats,bruno2002sit,elhelw2009statadvisor}.

Installing a statistic, however, does not guarantee its use in every
eligible estimate. Overlapping objects may compete for the same
predicates: applying one can make another inapplicable in that
estimation step. A priority or tie-breaking rule then affects the
information actually used. Even with fixed data and installed statistics, an advisor that
does not know the downstream selection rule may therefore evaluate
the same set against different complete estimates. We call the advisor's inability to determine the resulting
complete scheme \emph{estimation-scheme uncertainty}. It may arise
from an unknown deterministic application policy or intrinsic
randomization; the existence of several admissible schemes alone
does not establish uncertainty for an advisor that predicts the
selection exactly.

Consider the restricted example in Figure~\ref{fig:path}.
Three pairwise objects cover \(AB\), \(BC\), and \(CD\);
shared endpoints represent conflicts. If a complete scheme is
an inclusion-maximal matching, the installed set admits
\(\{s_{AB},s_{CD}\}\) and \(\{s_{BC}\}\).
Considering \(BC\) first selects the latter; considering
\(AB\) first selects the former. Suppose their modeled
task losses are \(0.1\) and \(2.0\), respectively.
Adding \(BC\) to an installed set containing \(AB,CD\)
thus introduces a higher-loss complete scheme without
removing the lower-loss option. This is a mathematical
example, not a PostgreSQL measurement.

Unlike sampling error or Train-to-Test workload generalization,
scheme uncertainty can persist with fixed data, sample,
workload, and resources. It can reflect incomplete policy
knowledge or an intrinsically stochastic selector; neither
randomness nor a difference in losses across schemes is required.

The advisor chooses an unordered installed set \(M\); for each task
\(i\), that set induces a prescribed family of \emph{complete}
admissible schemes \(\Gamma_i(M)\). A downstream policy chooses
the applied member. Nominal evaluation uses a specified policy,
whereas PESS evaluates each task's worst admissible scheme.
If one policy must serve all tasks, those separate worst schemes
may be mutually incompatible. Thus policy knowledge changes
the resource-selection objective, not merely the search heuristic.

We address three questions. \textbf{RQ1:} Does a controlled
priority intervention change the MCV objects PostgreSQL applies
and the resulting physical estimates? \textbf{RQ2:} How do
Nominal and PESS select resources under the same budget, and
how do shared-priority constraints change modeled risk?
\textbf{RQ3:} How do the frozen selections compare under a
declared \emph{counterfactual} distribution of physical
global relative-OID orders?

Our contributions are fourfold: (i) a general ESS formulation separating resource decisions,
complete admissible schemes, joint scheme uncertainty,
workload aggregation, and risk evaluation;
(ii) constructive shared-priority feasibility, a tight
conservatism bound, and restricted ordering hardness in a
labeled matching instance; (iii) a conditional PostgreSQL 16.14
source-level correspondence supported by controlled traces;
and (iv) frozen Train-model and physical Test evidence,
including an actual scheme-space witness and query-level diagnostics.
Interacting statistics and alternative complete estimation paths
have established precedents
\cite{bruno2004conditional,chen2022ceg,elhelw2009statadvisor}.
Our focus is narrower: \emph{upstream resource selection when the
advisor cannot reliably predict the downstream scheme choice}.
We do not claim a general robust optimizer or universally better
PESS selections.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Tasks, resources and maintenance}
Let \(\Tasks\) be a finite family of cardinality-estimation tasks.
A task identifies a relational cardinality target and its estimation
context; it need not be an entire SQL query or a physical plan node.
Its true cardinality is \(N_i\geq 0\), positive reference target is
\(T_i=\max\{1,N_i\}\), and workload weight is \(w_i>0\).
Let \(\E\) be a finite universe of estimation resources. The advisor
chooses one \emph{unordered} global set \(M\subseteq\E\), not an
application order or a separate scheme for each task.

Let \(\Maint:2^\E\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\) be a
modeled set cost, which may account for maintenance,
storage, collection, or an application-specific resource
constraint. Its accounting convention can depend on fixed
external data state and refresh horizon. At budget
\(B\geq0\), the feasible family is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:feasible}
\mathcal F_B=\{M\subseteq\E:\Maint(M)\leq B\}.
\end{equation}
We assume \(\mathcal F_B\ne\varnothing\).
The generic cost need not be additive, monotone,
submodular, or normalized to zero at the empty set.
The empty resource set still admits a baseline estimation
scheme, but need not be budget-feasible under every
accounting convention. Section~\ref{sec:protocol} introduces the much narrower
per-object \emph{proxy} used for the reported selections.

\subsection{Complete schemes and admissibility}
Each task \(i\) has a finite universe \(\Omega_i\) of \emph{complete}
estimation schemes. A scheme \(\sigma\) specifies enough context to
produce an estimate, possibly including the used statistics, their
predicate coverage and estimator-specific decisions. It need not be a
resource subset, matching or execution plan. Define
\(\operatorname{used}(\sigma)\subseteq\E\), and prescribe
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gamma}
\Gamma_i:2^\E\longrightarrow 2^{\Omega_i}\setminus\{\varnothing\},
\qquad
\sigma\in\Gamma_i(M)\Rightarrow
\operatorname{used}(\sigma)\subseteq M.
\end{equation}
A baseline scheme is available when \(M=\varnothing\).
The family \(\Gamma_i(M)\) describes the estimator's modeled
options, \emph{not} a second decision independently controlled
by the advisor. It may be nonnested as \(M\) grows:
installing a new resource can make a previously complete
scheme incomplete. For example, in the matching instance,
\(M=\{s_{AB}\}\) admits only \(\{s_{AB}\}\), but adding
the disjoint edge \(s_{CD}\) makes only
\(\{s_{AB},s_{CD}\}\) maximal. The old resource remains
installed, yet its formerly complete scheme is no longer
admissible. Nonnested scheme families do \emph{not} by
themselves imply nonmonotone losses; the losses assigned to
the resulting schemes and the policy rule also matter.

Crucially, \(\Gamma_i(M)\) is \emph{prescribed}, not
identified with the physically realizable family
\(\mathcal R_i(M)\). Neither equality nor containment of
\(\mathcal R_i(M)\) in \(\Gamma_i(M)\) follows by definition;
physical worst-case guarantees require separate evidence
for scheme coverage and loss alignment.

\subsection{Estimates, loss and application policies}
In general ESS, each complete scheme has a prescribed
nonnegative task loss \(L_i:\Omega_i\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}\).
The framework does not require a particular estimator
output range or loss formula. For our modeled instance,
a positive pre-output-transform estimate
\(\operatorname{Est}_i:\Omega_i\to\mathbb R_{>0}\)
yields weighted absolute logarithmic loss:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
L_i(\sigma)=w_i\ell_i(\sigma)
=w_i\left|\log\frac{\operatorname{Est}_i(\sigma)}{T_i}\right|.
\end{equation}
This instantiated model loss is distinct from the
rounded physical \texttt{Plan Rows} Q-error on Test.

A nonempty finite family \(\Theta\) of application-policy parameters
may induce a complete scheme \(\sigma_i(M;\theta)\) for each task,
installed set and policy. When the model and policy family are
compatible, \(\sigma_i(M;\theta)\in\Gamma_i(M)\) for all
relevant \(i,M,\theta\). One \(\theta\) shared among tasks couples their
scheme choices; it need not be probabilistic.

\subsection{Joint scheme uncertainty and risk}
\label{sec:joint-framework}
For a finite task sample indexed by $[n]$, a \emph{joint scheme
uncertainty model} prescribes a nonempty family
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:joint-family}
\mathcal U_n(M)\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_i(M)
\quad (M\in\mathcal F_B).
\end{equation}
It specifies which complete schemes may occur \emph{together},
without assuming independent choices across tasks. Its induced
set of modeled loss vectors is
\[
\mathcal L_n(M)=\{(L_i(\sigma_i))_{i=1}^n:
  \boldsymbol\sigma\in\mathcal U_n(M)\}.
\]
A workload aggregator $\mathcal A_n:\mathbb R_{\geq0}^n
\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}$ evaluates a given joint realization;
it is coordinatewise nondecreasing. Examples are a weighted
sum, sample mean, and empirical quantile. The set of attainable
aggregate values is
\[
\mathcal V_n(M)=
\{\mathcal A_n(\boldsymbol\ell):
\boldsymbol\ell\in\mathcal L_n(M)\}.
\]
A \emph{scheme-risk rule} $\rho$ evaluates the possible
aggregate losses, optionally with a specified probability law:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:unified-ess}
\min_{M\in\mathcal F_B} F_n(M),\qquad
F_n(M)=\rho(\mathcal V_n(M),\nu_M).
\end{equation}
For set-based worst-case risk, $\nu_M$ is omitted and
$\rho(\mathcal V_n)=\max\mathcal V_n$.
A probabilistic objective instead specifies a probability
measure $Q_M$ on the \emph{joint schemes}
$\mathcal U_n(M)$. Its push-forward $\nu_M$ is the law of
$\mathcal A_n((L_i(\sigma_i))_i)$ when
$\boldsymbol\sigma\sim Q_M$, and expectation risk is
$\rho(\mathcal V_n,\nu_M)=\int v\,d\nu_M(v)$.
Thus two policies with the same support but different
probabilities can have different risks. In particular,
uniform orders need not give uniform probabilities to
distinct scheme outcomes. Probability is \emph{additional
model data}; an admissibility set by itself implies no
distribution. Risk is assumed finite and well defined.
We require coordinatewise loss monotonicity of
$\mathcal A_n$, but do not impose a universal set-inclusion
monotonicity axiom on $\rho$: comparison of different
probability laws needs an explicitly chosen order or coupling.

For additive aggregation, the next subsection spells out
three objectives. A stable reference selection produces a
singleton joint family, a shared policy $\theta\in\Theta$
produces the image of its joint response map, and task-wise
PESS permits the full Cartesian product of admissible
schemes. These models need not coincide: individual
scheme realizability does not imply joint realizability
by one common policy.

\paragraph{What the uncertainty model asserts.}
The joint family is a \emph{prescription} about possible
cross-task applications, not a conclusion drawn from the
marginal admissible families alone. In particular, two
models can agree on every $\Gamma_i(M)$ but disagree on
$\mathcal U_n(M)$: one may allow independently chosen
schemes, while another restricts the tuple to the image
of a shared policy. A shared-policy model with stable
semantics has a common parameter space $\Theta$ and
responses $\sigma_i(M;\theta)$ for each $M$. It must
satisfy $\sigma_i(M;\theta)\in\Gamma_i(M)$, but the
policy image need not cover every admissible scheme.
Even if each scheme is individually realized by
some $\theta$, independently requested task schemes
may not be jointly realizable by any \emph{one} $\theta$.
These are properties of the specified uncertainty model,
not assumptions about every DBMS.

\paragraph{Ambiguity, uncertainty, and sensitivity.}
The condition $|\Gamma_i(M)|>1$ expresses
\emph{scheme ambiguity}, not automatically
uncertainty for an advisor that can predict the
selection perfectly. Scheme uncertainty may instead
arise from incomplete policy information, stochastic
selection, or both. It need not cause different
modeled losses: schemes can have equal estimates or
equal losses. Likewise, a positive fixed-set risk gap
need not change the optimal resource decision. Hence
we do not require observed loss or decision sensitivity
as part of the ESS definition; their presence is an
instance-specific question.

\paragraph{Scope of monotonicity.}
A coordinatewise loss increase cannot improve a
nondecreasing workload aggregator. This statement is
different from asserting that adding an admissible
scenario raises every kind of scheme risk, or that
installing more statistics improves the objective.
The worst-case rule does satisfy the former
\emph{scenario-set} inclusion property; a probabilistic
mean cannot be ordered from its support alone.
Neither rule gives a universal resource-set
monotonicity guarantee because $\Gamma_i(M)$ and
$\mathcal U_n(M)$ can change nonnestedly.

\begin{proposition}[Worst-case uncertainty relaxation]
\label{prop:general-relaxation}
Fix $M$ and common task losses and workload aggregator.
For nonempty joint families
$\mathcal U_n^{(1)}(M)\subseteq\mathcal U_n^{(2)}(M)$,
their worst-case aggregate losses satisfy
$F_n^{(1)}(M)\leq F_n^{(2)}(M)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The first family's aggregated loss image is a subset of the
second's. A maximum over a subset cannot exceed the maximum
over the full set.
\end{proof}
This only compares \emph{fixed-set modeled risk}. A strict
risk gap need not change the globally optimal resource set;
different greedy selections do not certify distinct optima.

\begin{proposition}[Task-wise envelope for monotone aggregation]
\label{prop:general-envelope}
For a fixed feasible $M$, put
$p_i(M)=\max_{\sigma\in\Gamma_i(M)}L_i(\sigma)$.
If $\mathcal A_n$ is coordinatewise nondecreasing, then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:general-envelope}
\max_{\boldsymbol\sigma\in\mathcal U_n(M)}
 \mathcal A_n((L_i(\sigma_i))_i)
\ \leq\ \mathcal A_n((p_i(M))_i).
\end{equation}
Equality holds when $\mathcal U_n(M)$ is the full
Cartesian product $\prod_i\Gamma_i(M)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every jointly admissible tuple and every $i$,
$L_i(\sigma_i)\leq p_i(M)$; coordinatewise monotonicity
gives the inequality. Since each $\Gamma_i(M)$ is
finite and nonempty, a maximizer of $L_i$ exists for
every task. Their joint tuple belongs to the full
Cartesian product and attains the right-hand side.
\end{proof}
Thus task-wise pessimism is an \emph{envelope} for
every such aggregator, not just the sum. In particular,
the aggregate may be a sample percentile, yet
(\ref{eq:general-envelope}) remains a \emph{modeled}
upper bound only; it says nothing about an unknown
physical scheme family. If the aggregator is not
coordinatewise monotone, the bound need not hold.
For additive aggregation, the right-hand side reduces
to $\sum_i p_i(M)$, recovering (\ref{eq:pess}).
For a percentile, it is the percentile of the
\emph{task-wise worst losses}, not necessarily the
worst physical percentile under one shared policy.

\subsection{Concrete policy-information assumptions}
For a fixed \(M\in\mathcal F_B\) and compatible nonempty finite
policy family \(\Theta\), the following are distinct objectives:
\begin{align}
F_{\Nom}(M;\theta_0)
 &=\sum_{i\in\Tasks}L_i(\sigma_i(M;\theta_0)),\label{eq:nom}\\
F_{\mathrm{joint}}(M)
 &=\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{i\in\Tasks}
 L_i(\sigma_i(M;\theta)),\label{eq:joint}\\
F_{\PESS}(M)
 &=\sum_{i\in\Tasks}\max_{\sigma\in\Gamma_i(M)}
 L_i(\sigma).\label{eq:pess}
\end{align}
The advisor's optimization is \(\min_{M\in\mathcal F_B}F(M)\)
for its chosen information assumption. The advisor does not
simultaneously choose the worst-case scheme or its application
policy. For the \emph{same fixed} \(M\), compatibility and common
loss imply
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:genericbound}
F_{\Nom}(M;\theta_0)\leq
F_{\mathrm{joint}}(M)\leq F_{\PESS}(M).
\end{equation}
The second inequality may be strict because separate taskwise
worst schemes may not be jointly attainable.

These objectives express \emph{different information
assumptions}, not alternative implementations of a known
policy. A stable, evaluable reference behavior supports Nominal
evaluation, without requiring the advisor to know its internals;
an unknown but shared rule motivates worst-joint risk. PESS discards that shared-rule constraint. A stochastic
average requires a separately justified policy distribution:
an unknown deterministic rule alone supplies none.
The restricted global-order instance makes these distinctions
concrete. It separates a \emph{shared rule across tasks}
from taskwise choices that no single rule can realize.

\paragraph{Risk envelopes do not rank resource decisions.}
The inequality in (\ref{eq:genericbound}) holds at the
\emph{same} resource set. It does not imply that the
resource set minimizing PESS also minimizes Nominal, that
the two argmin sets are disjoint, or that their physical
losses have any particular ordering. Consider a two-task
abstract instance with feasible choices $M_a,M_b$ and
loss tuples under two shared policies as follows:
under $M_a$, the policy outcomes have losses $(0,0)$
and $(2,2)$; under $M_b$, both policies give $(1,1)$.
Take the first policy as the Nominal reference and let
each task's admissible family be precisely its outcomes
under the two policies. Then
\[
F_{\Nom}(M_a)=0,\quad F_{\PESS}(M_a)=4,\qquad
F_{\Nom}(M_b)=F_{\PESS}(M_b)=2.
\]
The unique Nominal minimizer is $M_a$, whereas the
unique PESS minimizer is $M_b$. Yet if both policy
outcomes at $M_a$ instead have loss $(0,0)$, the
objectives share a minimizer. Thus \emph{decision
sensitivity} is possible but not necessary. This
example is an abstract feasible decision table, not
a claim that those outcomes arise from the restricted
PostgreSQL mechanism. In contrast, the matching
instance in Section~\ref{sec:pg} supplies the concrete
scheme-application structure studied in this paper.

\paragraph{Aggregation before policy averaging.}
The order of operations matters. For a declared distribution
over shared policies, averaging the workload aggregate
need not agree with aggregating per-task expected losses:
\[
\mathbb E_\theta[\mathcal A_n((L_i(\sigma_i(M;\theta)))_i)]
\ne
\mathcal A_n((\mathbb E_\theta L_i(\sigma_i(M;\theta)))_i)
\]
in general. For example, let two equally likely policies
produce the two-task loss vectors $(10,0)$ and $(0,10)$.
With $\mathcal A_2$ equal to the maximum, the left side
is $10$, whereas the right side is $5$. Thus an expected
per-policy maximum is not the maximum of expected
per-task losses. The same lack of interchangeability
can arise with empirical quantiles. In Section~\ref{sec:protocol},
the counterfactual physical experiment estimates the
expectation over orders of \emph{per-order Test P95};
it does not pool query--order observations before
computing a percentile.

\subsection{Population objectives and empirical optimization}
\label{sec:population}
For a population interpretation, let future tasks $X$ follow
an unknown fixed distribution $\mathcal D$, and let the
training tasks $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be IID draws. The population
risk and its empirical counterpart must use the \emph{same}
uncertainty assumptions, scheme-level loss, and workload-risk
definition for an empirical-to-population generalization claim.
For additive average loss, two distinct examples are
\begin{align}
\mathcal R_{\rm shared}(M)
 &=\max_{\theta\in\Theta}
 \mathbb E_{X\sim\mathcal D}
 [L_X(\sigma_X(M;\theta))],\label{eq:pop-shared}\\
\mathcal R_{\rm PESS}(M)
 &=\mathbb E_{X\sim\mathcal D}
 [\max_{\sigma\in\Gamma_X(M)}L_X(\sigma)].
 \label{eq:pop-pess}
\end{align}
Their sample counterparts replace each expectation by
the average over $X_1,\dots,X_n$, \emph{without moving}
the maximization across the expectation. We assume these
population risks are finite on $\mathcal F_B$. General
non-additive aggregators require a separately specified
population functional; e.g., a population quantile is not
simply the expected empirical quantile.

The separation between statistical and computational error
is exact. Let $\widehat{\mathcal R}_n$ estimate a specified
$\mathcal R$ on the same finite feasible family, and let
$\widetilde M_n\in\mathcal F_B$ be any algorithm output.
Then
\begin{align}
\label{eq:excess-decomposition}
&\mathcal R(\widetilde M_n)-\min_{M\in\mathcal F_B}
\mathcal R(M)\nonumber\\
&\ \leq
2\sup_{M\in\mathcal F_B}
|\widehat{\mathcal R}_n(M)-\mathcal R(M)|
+\widehat{\mathcal R}_n(\widetilde M_n)
-\min_{M\in\mathcal F_B}\widehat{\mathcal R}_n(M).
\end{align}
The first term measures same-objective statistical discrepancy;
the second is empirical optimization error, which need not
vanish for an add-only greedy algorithm.

\begin{proposition}[Consistency under finite shared policies]
\label{prop:finite-policy-consistency}
Suppose $\mathcal F_B$ and $\Theta$ are nonempty and finite,
the mappings $\sigma_X(M;\theta)$ are measurable, and
$\mathbb E L_X(\sigma_X(M;\theta))<\infty$ for every
$M\in\mathcal F_B$ and $\theta\in\Theta$.
For the shared-policy objective in
(\ref{eq:pop-shared}), its empirical counterpart converges
uniformly over $\mathcal F_B$ almost surely.
Any exact empirical minimizer consequently has population
excess risk converging to zero almost surely.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The strong law of large numbers applies to each fixed
$(M,\theta)$. There are finitely many such pairs, so their
maximum absolute empirical-to-population deviation tends
to zero almost surely. The inequality
$|\max_\theta a_\theta-\max_\theta b_\theta|
\leq\max_\theta|a_\theta-b_\theta|$
gives uniform convergence of shared-policy risk over
$\mathcal F_B$. Equation~(\ref{eq:excess-decomposition})
with zero empirical optimization error proves the result.
\end{proof}
\begin{proposition}[Task-wise pessimistic consistency]
\label{prop:pess-consistency}
Suppose $\mathcal F_B$ is finite and nonempty, each
$X\mapsto \max_{\sigma\in\Gamma_X(M)}L_X(\sigma)$
is measurable and integrable for every feasible $M$,
and $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are IID from $\mathcal D$.
The empirical version of (\ref{eq:pop-pess})
converges uniformly over $\mathcal F_B$ almost surely.
Consequently, an exact empirical PESS minimizer has
vanishing population excess PESS risk almost surely.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For each fixed $M$, apply the strong law to the scalar
random variable
$h_M(X)=\max_{\sigma\in\Gamma_X(M)}L_X(\sigma)$.
Because $\mathcal F_B$ is finite, the maximum of the
absolute empirical-to-population deviations over its
members tends to zero almost surely. Applying
(\ref{eq:excess-decomposition}) to an exact empirical
minimizer completes the proof.
\end{proof}
These are consistency statements for \emph{matched}
modeled population and empirical targets, not claims
about a general rate or an optimization guarantee for
the greedy solver. Shared-policy consistency with an
infinite $\Theta$ needs additional uniform convergence
assumptions; the sample-wise PESS maximization in this
proposition instead produces one integrable task loss.

Neither consistency result establishes a general finite-sample
rate, consistency for unrestricted policy classes, nor
generalization of the physical PostgreSQL Test P95.
In our experiments, modeled training loss and physical
\texttt{Plan Rows} Q-error also differ: evaluation under
a counterfactual OID-order distribution is a distinct
cross-model assessment, not the empirical counterpart of
(\ref{eq:pop-shared}) or (\ref{eq:pop-pess}).

\paragraph{From general ESS to the structured instance.}
The abstract formulation does not require schemes to be
matchings or policies to be orders. Sections~\ref{sec:pg}
and~\ref{sec:objectives} instantiate these objects in two
steps. First, under A1--A4, a task's prescribed family
$\Gamma_i(M)$ becomes the set of inclusion-maximal
matchings of the installed eligible two-column MCV
graph $H_i[M]$. Each matching is a complete modeled
scheme; its multiplicative estimate and loss are
separate numerical assumptions. Second, a strict
global edge order $\pi$ induces one matching per task.
Holding that order common across tasks specifies a
coupled joint family, whereas taking the full product
of task-wise maximal matchings specifies PESS.
Consequently, the general fixed-set uncertainty
relaxation and task-wise envelope apply to this
instance, but they do not by themselves characterize
which tuples a shared order can realize. That question
requires the matching arguments in
Section~\ref{sec:objectives}. The source correspondence
does not make abstract priorities controllable in a
fixed physical PostgreSQL catalog or identify modeled
losses with Test Q-error.

\section{A Scoped PostgreSQL MCV Mechanism}
\label{sec:pg}
We first identify the restricted source-level selector and its
prerequisites, define the corresponding maximal-matching model,
then study the resulting abstract priority choices. None of these structural results identifies its
multiplicative numerical loss with physical PostgreSQL Q-error.

\subsection{Version-pinned selector and its prerequisites}
PostgreSQL 16.14's single-relation selectivity path can apply
multivariate MCV objects to still-unestimated compatible
clauses. Source inspection covers four selector functions:\\
{\raggedright
\texttt{clauselist\_selectivity\_ext},\\
\texttt{statext\_mcv\_clauselist\_selectivity},\\
\texttt{choose\_best\_statistics}, and\\
\texttt{RelationGetStatExtList}.\par}
The selector repeats candidate selection over a source-level
OID-sorted candidate list. Under the conditions below,
all surviving eligible two-key objects tie in the ranking,
so list/OID order resolves which object is used first
\cite{pg16stats}. This is a deterministic mechanism
for a fixed catalog, not a source of random orders.
The version-pinned code locations and conditional argument
are recorded in the accompanying project
\href{https://github.com/1951123/ess-research/blob/main/notes/pg-mcv-maximal-matching-correspondence.md}
{source-correspondence note}.

The correspondence assumes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{A1.}] Each relevant clause is a supported simple MCV
  predicate on exactly one distinct base-table attribute. It is
  non-pseudoconstant, with one relevant clause per attribute and no OR,
  expression or repeated-attribute predicates.
\item[\textbf{A2.}] Eligible objects are \emph{built}
  two-attribute, no-expression MCV statistics on the same
  base relation, covering two currently unestimated
  compatible attribute clauses.
\item[\textbf{A3.}] Privilege, relation and inheritance checks
  permit the objects, no other estimator preconsumes those
  clauses, and the candidate list is exactly the
  installed eligible set.
\item[\textbf{A4.}] Every object whose two endpoints remain
  free ties under the selector's key/coverage ranking;
  the fixed list order breaks the tie.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Restricted matching model and scheme loss}
For the structured instance, each candidate \(e\in\E\) is a
\emph{labeled} two-attribute MCV object, with endpoints
\(A_e\), \(|A_e|=2\). For each task \(i\), statically
eligible objects \(\E_i\subseteq\E\) form the edge-labeled
multigraph \(H_i=(V_i,\E_i)\). The installed eligible graph
\(H_i[M]\) contains the edges \(M\cap\E_i\). Distinct
statistics with identical attribute pairs remain distinct edges.
A matching consists of pairwise endpoint-disjoint edges.
We prescribe
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mm}
\Gamma_i^{\mathrm{MM}}(M)=\MM(H_i[M]).
\end{equation}
\emph{Maximal} means no additional available edge can be appended;
it does not mean maximum cardinality. Even an empty graph has
the empty matching as its unique maximal matching.

For a matching \(S\), positive baseline pre-clamp
estimate \(n_i^0>0\), and fixed task-specific correction
factors \(r_{e,i}>0\), the controlled multiplicative
approximation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:multiplicative}
\widehat n_i(S)=n_i^0\prod_{e\in S}r_{e,i},
\quad
L_i(S)=w_i\Bigl|\alpha_i+\sum_{e\in S}b_{e,i}\Bigr|,
\end{equation}
where \(\alpha_i=\log(n_i^0/T_i)\) and
\(b_{e,i}=\log r_{e,i}\).
Positivity is necessary before taking logarithms.
This separability is \emph{assumed for the model}, not a theorem
about physical PostgreSQL MCV estimation or independence of
underlying attributes. In the actual frozen study, the
\texttt{census13-clamped-log-v2} and related dataset-specific
coefficient constructions follow pinned normalization and
quantization rules, with exact \texttt{Fraction} reference loss.
The physical \texttt{Plan Rows} metric is evaluated separately.




An edge is labeled by the particular built MCV object, not
just its attribute pair: distinct objects on the same pair
can have different contents. Under A1--A3, applying an edge
uses the compatible clauses for both endpoint attributes.
Those clauses are then marked estimated, so an edge sharing
either endpoint cannot also be applied to the remaining
clauses in that estimation step. This is the graph conflict
represented by a shared endpoint. A1--A4 make the available
unconsumed edges exactly the candidates inspected by the
repeated selector, with tied candidates resolved by its fixed
list order. At termination, an unselected edge disjoint from
all selected edges would still cover two free clauses and
would remain eligible, contradicting termination. Hence the
selected objects form an inclusion-wise maximal matching.
For the path in Figure~\ref{fig:path}, one such matching has
two edges and another has one: maximality means no edge can
be appended, not that the matching has maximum cardinality.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional source correspondence]
\label{prop:pg}
Under A1--A4, the selected labeled two-column MCV
objects constitute an inclusion-maximal matching of \(H_i[M]\).
If the abstract tie priority is freely varied over all strict
total orders, its attainable \emph{single-task} outputs
are exactly \(\Gamma_i^{\mathrm{MM}}(M)\).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
After selecting a two-attribute object, the corresponding
clauses are marked estimated; subsequent selected objects
cannot share either endpoint. If an unselected edge were
disjoint from every selected edge at termination, its two
clauses would remain eligible, contradicting the terminating
failure to find a candidate. The output is maximal.
Conversely, Proposition~\ref{prop:completion}
orders any chosen maximal matching first.
\end{proof}
The proposition characterizes the restricted selector
under A1--A4, \emph{not} arbitrary SQL, joins,
other statistics kinds, competing clause consumers, or
non-tied rankings. In a fixed physical catalog the OID
order is not freely variable. Thus the correspondence
supplies a concrete structural instance of the ESS model,
not an assertion that PostgreSQL implements ESS or
realizes every abstract priority without catalog changes.

\subsection{Physical mechanism evidence}
A controlled four-attribute trace with objects \(AB,BC,CD\)
used an instrumented PostgreSQL development build. Creating
objects in order \(AB,BC,CD\) produced the selected set
\(\{AB,CD\}\), while \(BC,AB,CD\) produced
\(\{BC\}\). Both terminated without an eligible candidate
and involved built MCV data. This was a scoped fixture-level
observation; recreating objects can otherwise affect sample
contents.

In a later, content-controlled held-out order intervention,
four frozen Nominal \(B=5\) sets were recreated in canonical
or exact-reverse order. Checks fixed the data, replayed
ANALYZE sample, settings and MCV-content fingerprints.
All 80,000 Test EXPLAIN outcomes and MCV diagnostics
completed. Section~\ref{sec:results} quantifies changed
applied-object sequences and actual row-estimation effects.
Neither intervention makes all maximal matchings physically
attainable in every context, or equates the multiplicative
model with PostgreSQL's entire cardinality calculation.

\section{Structural Properties of the Restricted Instance}
\label{sec:objectives}

Section~\ref{sec:model} defines arbitrary joint scheme
uncertainty and loss aggregation; Section~\ref{sec:pg}
supplies one conditional matching-based scheme family.
Here we analyze the \emph{additional coupling} imposed
when all tasks obey a common strict priority. The
resulting $G(M)$ is a special case of general
shared-policy worst risk, while $P(M)$ is the additive
task-wise envelope. In particular, the general bound
$G(M)\leq P(M)$ requires only admissibility and
nonnegative additive losses; the realizability test,
tight factor $t$, and fixed-set hardness below use
the labeled matching and free-order structure.
All comparisons remain at fixed $M$ unless explicitly
stated otherwise.

\subsection{One shared global priority}
For the pairwise instance, let \(\Pi(M)\) contain all strict
permutations of the \emph{labeled selected resources}, including
irrelevant edges. The same order \(\pi\) applies to every task,
restricted to that task's eligible installed edges. A greedy scan
accepts an edge if both endpoints are unused and produces
\(\gamma_i(M,\pi)\in\Gamma_i^{\mathrm{MM}}(M)\).
Let \(Z(M,\pi)=\sum_iL_i(\gamma_i(M,\pi))\) and define
\begin{align}
N_{\pi_0}(M)&=Z(M,\pi_0),&
A(M)&=\frac{\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(M)}Z(M,\pi)}{|\Pi(M)|},
\label{eq:na}\\
G(M)&=\max_{\pi\in\Pi(M)}Z(M,\pi),&
P(M)&=\sum_i\max_{Q\in\Gamma_i^{\mathrm{MM}}(M)}L_i(Q).
\label{eq:gp}
\end{align}
The fixed \(\pi_0\) is the restriction of the candidate-ID
order used in the frozen study. Uniform \(A\) weights
\emph{orders}, not their potentially repeated matching outcomes.
The same order in \(G\) couples tasks even when their eligible
graphs differ; \(P\) instead takes an independent maximum
before summing, so its maximizing schemes may be incompatible
under any shared priority.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-set global-order bounds]
\label{prop:bounds}
For every fixed \(M\) in the prescribed pairwise model and the same
finite task losses,
\[
A(M)\leq G(M)\leq P(M),\qquad N_{\pi_0}(M)\leq G(M).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The mean and any particular member of the finite
collection \(\{Z(M,\pi)\}\) do not exceed its maximum.
For any \(\pi\) and task \(i\), greedy produces an admissible
maximal matching, whose loss is at most that task's maximal
admissible loss. Summing and maximizing over \(\pi\) proves
\(G(M)\leq P(M)\).
\end{proof}
These inequalities compare \emph{modeled loss on the same fixed set}.
Neither \(A(M)\) nor \(N_{\pi_0}(M)\) universally dominates
the other. They do not rank separately selected resource sets or
bound physical PostgreSQL Q-error without additional assumptions.

\begin{proposition}[Single-task order completeness]
\label{prop:completion}
For a fixed task graph \(H_i[M]\), every inclusion-maximal
matching is produced by greedy under some freely chosen
abstract total edge priority.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put the matching's edges first. Since those edges are disjoint,
greedy accepts them all. By inclusion-maximality every remaining
edge meets an accepted edge, so none is subsequently accepted.
\end{proof}
This proves neither cross-task simultaneous completeness nor
physical realizability of every abstract ordering within
a fixed PostgreSQL catalog.

\subsection{A strict coupling gap and nonmonotone example}
On the path in Figure~\ref{fig:path}, four of the six global
permutations select \(\{s_{AB},s_{CD}\}\), whereas two select
\(\{s_{BC}\}\). Uniform orders therefore weight the two
matchings \(2/3\) and \(1/3\), not \(1/2\) each.
For two tasks with the \emph{same} path and losses
\(L_1(Q_1)=1,L_1(Q_2)=0\) but
\(L_2(Q_1)=0,L_2(Q_2)=1\), every shared priority yields
total loss one; the independent taskwise maxima sum to two:
\(A(M)=G(M)=1<P(M)=2\). This is an abstract witness to
conservatism in \(P\), not a dataset measurement.

Similarly, take the installed set \(\{s_{AB},s_{CD}\}\)
with sole complete matching \(Q_1\) of loss \(0.1\).
Adding the conflicting resource \(s_{BC}\) permits \(Q_2\)
of loss \(2.0\), so this particular PESS objective increases
from \(0.1\) to \(2.0\). Nonmonotonicity is thus possible,
rather than a general law that new statistics harm estimates.

\subsection{Shared-priority realizability and complexity}
\label{sec:shared-theory}
The coupling in \(G\) admits a constructive characterization.
Fix \(M\) and one inclusion-maximal matching \(S_i\) in each
task graph \(H_i[M]\). A common strict edge order realizes
all these prescribed matchings if and only if, for every
eligible \(e\notin S_i\), at least one target edge
\(f\in S_i\) that shares an endpoint with \(e\) precedes
\(e\). Necessity follows because a rejected edge must
have an earlier accepted blocker; sufficiency follows
by considering the first departure from any target matching.

The decision input explicitly lists a common set of $m$
labeled edges with endpoints, each task's eligible
edge IDs, and its prescribed target edge IDs $S_i$.
Invalid targets are rejected after checking that each
$S_i$ is an inclusion-maximal matching in its task
graph. Let $N$ be the length of this explicit input.
The condition gives a constructive polynomial-time test
\emph{for supplied targets}. Maintain a prefix $R$ of
labeled edges. An unplaced edge $e$ is \emph{safe} if,
for every task containing $e$, either $e\in S_i$ or
some $f\in S_i$ sharing an endpoint with $e$ is
already in $R$. Repeatedly append any safe edge.
If every edge is placed, the resulting order realizes
all $S_i$. If no safe edge remains, no common order
realizes the targets.

The safety condition must be checked against
\emph{every} task containing an edge: an edge may be
a target in one task and a non-target in another.
Suppose there exists a feasible extension of the
current prefix. Take a safe unplaced edge $e$ and move it to
the beginning of that extension. Whenever $e$ is a
target, it is disjoint from all other targets for
that task; whenever it is not a target, its required
target blocker is already in the prefix. Thus this
exchange preserves all desired greedy outcomes.
Induction shows that safe choices preserve feasibility.
Conversely, the first edge of any feasible remaining
suffix must be safe, so a dead end certifies
infeasibility. A naive implementation performs at most $m$ rounds,
scans at most $m$ edges per round, and checks
safety against the explicit input in $O(N)$ time,
giving an $O(m^2N)$ upper bound. This does not
cover succinctly encoded task families. This test does not compute $G(M)$ or
search for unspecified task-wise worst matchings.

\begin{proposition}[Tight task-count conservatism]
\label{prop:taskfactor}
For a fixed installed set \(M\), \(t\geq1\) tasks,
nonnegative modeled losses, and freely ordered
labeled maximal-matchings,
\[
G(M)\leq P(M)\leq tG(M).
\]
The factor \(t\) is tight.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For task \(i\), let \(p_i\) be its largest
admissible loss. A priority that begins with
a maximal matching attaining \(p_i\) realizes
that loss. Since other task losses are
nonnegative, \(G(M)\geq p_i\) for every \(i\).
Thus \(P(M)=\sum_i p_i\leq tG(M)\).

For tightness, let all $t$ tasks share a
$t$-edge star, with every edge eligible for every task.
Each maximal matching is a singleton. For task $i$,
set $\alpha_i=0$, $w_i=1$, and
$b_{e_i,i}=\ln 2$, $b_{e_j,i}=0$ for $j\ne i$.
Equation~(\ref{eq:multiplicative}) then gives loss
$\ln 2$ exactly when $e_i$ is selected and zero
otherwise. Thus $P=t\ln 2$, while any common
global order chooses the same first edge in every
task, giving $G=\ln 2$. This achieves the factor
within the stipulated positive multiplicative-loss
model, not just an arbitrary task-loss table.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-set shared-order hardness]
\label{prop:shared-hardness}
Computing $G(M)$ in the freely ordered labeled
matching model with loss (\ref{eq:multiplicative})
is NP-hard, even when each task has two eligible
conflicting edges and all correction factors are
in $\{1,2\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Reduce Maximum Acyclic Subgraph for a loopless
directed graph $D=(V,A_D)$
\cite{karp1972reducibility}. For each vertex $v$,
install one labeled edge $e_v$ in a common star.
For each arc $(u,v)\in A_D$, make a task eligible
for exactly $e_u,e_v$, with baseline estimate equal
to its positive target, unit workload weight, and
correction factors $r_{e_u,i}=2$ and $r_{e_v,i}=1$.
Its only maximal matchings select $e_u$ or $e_v$,
giving modeled losses $\ln 2$ and zero. Under a
shared strict priority $\pi$, greedy selects $e_u$
exactly when $u$ precedes $v$. Therefore
\[
\frac{G(M)}{\ln 2}=
\max_{\pi}|\{(u,v)\in A_D:u\text{ precedes }v\text{ under }\pi\}|.
\]
The right side is the maximum number of forward
arcs, equal to the optimum of Maximum Acyclic
Subgraph. The construction has polynomial size,
and the restricted decision language has an
integer threshold $K$, explicit task-edge incidence,
and rational factors from $\{1,2\}$. It asks
whether some shared order makes at least $K$
tasks incur $\ln 2$, i.e., $G(M)\geq K\ln 2$.
A proposed order is checked by counting forward
arcs using integer arithmetic, without general
transcendental-value comparisons. Thus this
restricted threshold problem is NP-complete;
computing the unrestricted fixed-set $G(M)$
is NP-hard. No claim of NP membership is
made for arbitrary real input representations.
\end{proof}
The hardness pertains to fixed-set evaluation
under freely variable \emph{abstract} priorities,
not outer resource-selection optimization or
physical PostgreSQL order control. It also does
not determine the complexity of testing
$P(M)=G(M)$ in general.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\subsection{Frozen resource-selection procedure}
The empirical selection trains \emph{only} \(N_{\pi_0}\)
and \(P\), using the same strictly improving, deterministic
add-only greedy heuristic, separately for each Train workload
and budget. Its feasibility is the experimental
\(\sum_{e\in M}\bar m_e\leq B\), with nonnegative
per-object \emph{proxy} costs. The heuristic adds the feasible
single resource giving the greatest strictly positive loss
decrease, breaking ties by candidate ID, and stops if no
such improvement exists. It performs no swaps or deletions
and does not certify global optimality. The read-only
offline \(A,G\) analysis is \emph{not} a retraining or
a different selection algorithm.


We distinguish effects on \emph{modeled resource decisions}
from changes in \emph{measured physical estimates}. Their
losses and provenance remain separate throughout.
\subsection{Evidence layers and frozen Train selection}
We separate \emph{Train-model resource decisions} from
\emph{physical held-out evaluation}. The original AreCEL
Forest10, Census13, DMV11, and Power7 workloads
\cite{wang2021arecel} each provide 100,000 Train tasks
for model construction and selection and 10,000 official
Test queries for PostgreSQL evaluation. Neither Test
labels nor physical Test estimates reselect resources.

The evidence has five complementary roles: (i) select
Nominal and PESS resource sets using Train data;
(ii) enumerate abstract priorities on these \emph{fixed}
sets; (iii) inspect one Census13 Train task's complete
scheme family; (iv) measure physical Test outcomes at
canonical and controlled reverse catalog orders; and
(v) sample relative-OID orders to estimate a
counterfactual order-population expectation. A subsequent
read-only paired-query analysis characterizes diagnostic
changes in the sampled physical observations.
Only the fifth stage averages physical Test P95 over
orders; its uncertainty concerns sampled orders,
not new queries or workloads.

The frozen Train selection chooses Nominal and PESS resources with
the same deterministic, strictly improving add-only greedy heuristic
at \(B\in\{0,2,3,4,5\}\). Each candidate's budget cost is one
\emph{proxy unit}, not measured maintenance CPU or storage. All 40
labeled decisions completed, giving 25 unique dataset-specific
physical configurations. The frozen resource manifest is stored at
\href{https://github.com/1951123/ess-research/blob/main/experiments/add-only-greedy-issue99/frozen-train-resources-issue96-v1.json}{the frozen resource-selection manifest}.
The frozen Train-model coefficients use positive clamped-log-derived
quantities and exact \texttt{Fraction} reference evaluation; they
are not physical \texttt{Plan Rows} measurements. Here
\emph{exact} refers to arithmetic checks of the frozen
\emph{modeled} losses and their workload sums against a pinned
reference, not to fidelity of the multiplicative approximation to
PostgreSQL, global optimality of the selected set, or a proof that
every intermediate operation of the selection implementation uses
rational arithmetic. For each fixed Train sample, the reported
additive model objectives are \emph{sums} of task losses;
dividing by its positive sample size converts them to empirical
averages as in Section~\ref{sec:population}, without changing
their minimizers or the order of strictly improving greedy
single-addition choices at that fixed sample size.

A read-only Census13 Train case study enumerates \(\Gamma_i(M)\)
for five existing B3/B4/B5 selected sets. It deterministically
takes the smallest stable Train task ID satisfying structural,
positive-risk and compact-family conditions. Exact task losses
were checked against a pinned advisor reference, and the
100,000-task objective sums agreed with the independent
frozen offline model summary. This is artifact agreement, not independent recollection
of raw Train data or a new resource-selection experiment.

\subsection{Physical PostgreSQL evaluation}
The fixed-order, reversal, and sampled-order physical
experiments use version-pinned PostgreSQL 16.14 with full
source relations, frozen ANALYZE sample replay, and effective
statistics target 300 for ordinary and selected extended
statistics. The order-intervention build is pinned
to source SHA \texttt{f1bf37b5dd3d3a7087fc0af5de550ca31035854c}.
Settings include these pinned values:\\
{\raggedright
\texttt{plan\_cache\_mode=force\_custom\_plan};\\
\texttt{max\_parallel\_workers\_per\_gather=0};\\
\texttt{client\_min\_messages=warning}.\par}
For controlled order changes,
the protocol verifies actual OID priority, full-table/sample identities,
ordinary statistics and individual MCV-content fingerprints.
Per-query extended-statistics diagnostics are used to verify applied
object and MCV-estimate sequences; EXPLAIN alone is insufficient.

For each original Test query, let \(t\geq0\) denote its truth
and \(e\geq0\) its rounded top-level \texttt{Plan Rows} estimate.
Using the frozen \(r=1\) rounding convention, the zero-aware
AreCEL Q-error is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qerror}
Q(t,e)=
\begin{cases}
1,&t=e=0,\\
t,&t>0,\ e=0,\\
e,&t=0,\ e>0,\\
\max(e/t,t/e),&t>0,\ e>0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The percentile implementation follows the original NumPy convention,
and differs from the selection model's absolute-log loss.
The canonical fixed-order evaluation completed 25 configurations
and 250,000 Test EXPLAINs. The controlled canonical/reverse
comparison completed eight configurations, 80,000 Test EXPLAINs and complete
selected-object/estimate diagnostics. Both studies use frozen,
previously selected resources.

\subsection{Offline Train-model global order enumeration}
The offline analysis enumerates strict global
priorities on the \emph{already selected} Train sets:
906 permutations in total for 25 distinct fixed sets. A global
order is shared across tasks and counted even when distinct
orders induce the same matching outcomes. It computes
\(N_{\pi_0}(M),A(M),G(M),P(M)\) using frozen
Train coefficients and exact reference checks, with
\emph{no} Test execution, refitting or reselection.
The enumeration code, frozen coefficients, exact summaries,
and verification tests accompany the reproducibility artifacts.

\subsection{Counterfactual uniform global relative-OID order}
For RQ3, define a \emph{hypothetical} uniform population of
the \(k!\) strict relative-OID orders of each fixed selected set
\(M\). One physical order is held unchanged for all
10,000 official Test queries in that arm. The primary estimand is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:estimand}
Y_\pi(M)=\operatorname{P95}_{q\in\mathrm{Test}}Q(q,M,\pi),
\qquad
\mu(M)=\mathbb E_{\pi\sim U(\Pi(M))}[Y_\pi(M)].
\end{equation}
It is the \emph{expected per-order Test P95}, not the P95
of order-averaged per-query Q-errors. Neither this assumption
nor the abstract global order distribution implies PostgreSQL
assigns OIDs randomly in production.

The sampled physical order study used \textbf{156 configurations},
not a complete 732-arm census. For each of four five-object
Nominal sets it included two canonical/reverse certainty orders
plus 22 simple-random-without-replacement (SRSWOR) orders
from the remaining 118. The two five-object PESS sets each
contributed 24 SRSWOR orders of 120. Two three-object PESS
sets contributed all six orders each. All 156 arms and
\textbf{1,560,000 cumulative Test EXPLAINs} eventually completed.

For Nominal let \(F\) be the mean of its two certainty outcomes,
\(\overline Y_R\) the mean of 22 residual sampled outcomes,
and \(s_R^2\) the usual sample variance. The design-weighted
estimate and variance are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:design}
\widehat\mu_N=\frac{2}{120}F+\frac{118}{120}\overline Y_R,
\quad
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\widehat\mu_N)=
\left(\frac{118}{120}\right)^2
\left(1-\frac{22}{118}\right)\frac{s_R^2}{22}.
\end{equation}
For five-object PESS, \(\widehat\mu_P=\overline Y_{24}\)
and the design variance estimate is \((1-24/120)s_P^2/24\).
The two three-object PESS means are exact six-order
population means conditional on the fixed Test split.
For the independently sampled PESS-minus-Nominal contrast,
the component variance estimates add. The frozen approximate
95\% intervals use \(t_{0.975,21}\) times the design standard
error. These are approximate \emph{design-based} intervals for
sampling orders from the stated finite order populations,
conditional on the frozen selected sets, Test queries, database
state, and ANALYZE replay. The use of a common
$t_{0.975,21}$ multiplier is a declared approximation for
the contrast, not an exact finite-population coverage theorem
for two components with different sampling designs.
They do not quantify uncertainty from new workloads,
datasets, ANALYZE samples, or the model-to-physical gap.
A degenerate interval
from 24 sampled orders cannot certify all 120 orders identical.

The sampled-order design was specified after inspection of
canonical and reversal outcomes, although new sampled orders
were fixed before their Test measurements. Eight earlier certainty
arms were remeasured after a reuse-eligibility failure whose
exact row-level cause was not preserved. Twelve three-object
PESS arms stopped before EXPLAIN because a validator expected
five objects; an authorized continuation completed those
\emph{same} frozen orders without replacements or extra arms.

\paragraph{Decomposing the modeled risk gap.}
For any fixed resource set, the full difference between
task-wise pessimism and the nominal reference separates
algebraically into two nonnegative components:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gap-decomposition}
P(M)-N_{\pi_0}(M)
=\underbrace{P(M)-G(M)}_{\text{cross-task relaxation}}
+\underbrace{G(M)-N_{\pi_0}(M)}_{\text{priority uncertainty}}.
\end{equation}
The first component is the price, in modeled loss,
of permitting each task to choose its worst matching
independently rather than enforcing a single common
global order. The second is the gap between that
common-order worst case and the one nominal priority.
Both use the \emph{same} installed set and the
\emph{same} Train loss model. This decomposition
does not imply that either component predicts
physical Test Q-error or that separately trained
Nominal and PESS resource sets can be compared by
subtracting their components. It also does not
attribute an observed physical change to an
individual statistic.

Table~\ref{tab:gap} isolates the coupling effect:
it measures \(P(M)-G(M)\) \emph{within each fixed B5 set}.
All four Nominal-selected sets have positive gaps,
meaning that independently maximizing task losses
exceeds what one common priority can jointly induce.
The four PESS-selected sets have zero gaps in this
frozen model. Neither pattern is a general theorem
about sets selected by the respective objectives,
and these Train-model gaps do not bound physical
Test Q-error.

\paragraph{Query-level diagnostics under frozen orders.}
A read-only analysis of the sampled-order physical records
validated per-query observations and reproduced the
reported aggregate estimates.
Across checked Nominal order pairs, every observed
Q-error change coincided with an MCV candidate-ID
sequence change, but the converse failed.
Relative to declared anchor orders, Census13 and DMV11
Nominal have 17,731 and 37,843 query--order pairs
with both changes. The corresponding PESS comparisons
have 87,921 and 89,890 changed ID sequences with
\emph{unchanged} Q-error and candidate-aligned numeric
MCV estimates. These are repeated, descriptive
query--order comparisons, not independent observations,
population estimates, or causal mediation tests;
the separate canonical/reverse pairs are excluded.
The physical observations thus separate \emph{application-order
sensitivity} from \emph{error sensitivity}. Diagnostics
locate differences among frozen configurations, but do not
identify causal effects of individual MCV applications.

The constant P95 observed for Census13's
24 sampled PESS orders does not establish
invariance across its 96 unsampled orders;
Forest10 and Power7 PESS instead have exact
six-order censuses. Finally, the exact offline Train
\(A(M)\) averages \emph{Train-model total
absolute-log loss} over abstract priorities,
while the sampled physical study averages \emph{Test
per-order P95 Q-error}. These different
metrics, splits, and mechanisms preclude
interpreting agreement of rankings as
model calibration.

\paragraph{Answers to the research questions.}
\emph{RQ1 (physical application sensitivity):}
Under the controlled canonical/reverse intervention on
the same frozen B5 Nominal sets, selected-MCV sequences
changed in thousands of paired Test queries. Physical
P95 changed as well, but its direction varied by
dataset; a changed applied sequence did not invariably
change query-level Q-error. This establishes order
sensitivity within the pinned experimental setting,
not universal physical sensitivity to every priority
or an isolated causal effect of one statistic.

\emph{RQ2 (decisions and modeled coupling):}
With identical proxy budgets and the same add-only
solver, Nominal and PESS selected different resource
IDs at some budgets, while they coincided at others.
For each \emph{fixed} selected set, the enumerated
$P(M)-G(M)$ measures the relaxation from one shared
priority to independently chosen task-wise worst
schemes; the gap decomposition in
(\ref{eq:gap-decomposition}) further distinguishes
shared-priority uncertainty from the Nominal
reference. Different greedy outputs do not prove
different globally optimal resource sets. These are
statements about frozen modeled Train losses rather
than physical Test risk.

\emph{RQ3 (conditional physical comparison):}
The canonical-order comparison favors Nominal at B5
on all four datasets. For the \emph{different} estimand
that averages per-order Test P95 across a specified
uniform relative-OID-order population, the estimated
contrast favors PESS on Census13 and DMV11, Nominal
on Forest10, and is unresolved on Power7 by the
reported approximate sampling interval. Neither
estimand is a measured production distribution.
Thus the two rankings answer distinct deployment
policy questions and are not contradictory.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:validity}
The main inferential boundary separates the prescribed
complete-scheme model from a version-pinned physical
implementation. The limitations below qualify distinct
claims rather than a single common outcome.
\paragraph{Prescribed model and physical coverage.}
The prescribed \(\Gamma_i(M)\) is not known to
contain all physically realizable schemes
\(\mathcal R_i(M)\); even containment would not
suffice for a physical-error bound without loss
alignment. The matching correspondence assumes A1--A4
and may fail for joins, other predicate patterns,
statistics kinds, or estimation stages. Scheme-family
nonnestedness alone does not imply nonmonotonicity
of every objective. The deterministically selected
Census13 Train witness demonstrates a possible
loss gap, not task-population representativeness
or general family nonnestedness.

\paragraph{Resource and metric proxies.}
The selected resource sets come from a strictly
improving add-only greedy heuristic, not a globally
optimal outer solver. Budget units proxy maintenance
rather than measured collection, refresh, storage,
or update overhead. Train-model absolute-log loss
differs from PostgreSQL Test Q-error; lower Q-error
does not itself establish better plan latency.
Neither maintenance savings nor runtime gains
are demonstrated here.

\paragraph{Order assumptions and inference.}
A fixed catalog has a deterministic OID order;
strategy uncertainty does not imply randomization.
The uniform abstract and physical relative-OID
order populations are \emph{counterfactual},
not learned deployment distributions.
The 156-arm study does not enumerate all
five-object orders; its approximate intervals
cover permutation sampling conditional on
the frozen Test split. A constant outcome among
24 sampled orders cannot certify the remaining
96. The order design followed inspection of
earlier experiments, and the record retains both
pre-EXPLAIN configuration failures and an
unresolved historical reuse-rejection cause.
The paired-query diagnostic counts do not
identify causal mediation.

\paragraph{Generalization and reproducibility.}
Results are limited to four original AreCEL
splits, two-column single-relation MCV resources,
statistics target 300, and the pinned
PostgreSQL 16.14 build; we do not reproduce
AreCEL's learned estimator \cite{wang2021arecel}.
Protocols, analysis code, findings, and hashes
are versioned, but query-level physical raw
observations and manifests are retained in
documented external runtime archives.
No cross-DBMS generalization, universal
PESS superiority, or unconditional physical
worst-case claim follows.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Statistics management and advisors.}
Budgeted statistics selection is established
research. Chaudhuri and Narasayya identify
essential statistics sets for a specified
optimizer plan/cost criterion, including
multicolumn objects, multiple valid sets,
and order-dependent shrinking outcomes
\cite{chaudhuri2000stats}. Their work already
demonstrates interactions in statistics
selection; ESS instead evaluates a resource
set against a family of downstream complete
applications when the selecting policy is
not dependably known.

Bruno and Chaudhuri's statistics for
intermediate query expressions (SITs)
already address uncertainty and sensitivity:
Min/Max hypothetical distributions yield
extreme selectivity and cardinality estimates,
used to assess plan-cost spread and candidate
SIT contributions \cite{bruno2002sit}.
Their uncertainty concerns distributions and
optimizer responses; ours concerns which
complete application the estimator selects
from installed resources. SITs concern
intermediate expressions, whereas our physical
instance uses single-table two-column MCVs.

StatAdvisor models dependencies and grouped
benefits among statistical views
\cite{elhelw2009statadvisor}; CORDS and
query-feedback approaches identify attribute
correlations for statistics recommendations
\cite{ilyas2004cords,haas2007dependencies}.
Physical-design relaxations also handle
interdependent choices across structures
\cite{bruno2005relaxation}. We claim no novelty
for budgeted selection, correlations, or
resource interactions in isolation.
The additional decision input in ESS is the
resource-dependent \emph{family of complete
applications} and the advisor's knowledge
of the policy choosing among them.

\paragraph{Alternative complete estimation paths.}
Conditional selectivity already treats
conflicting SITs and alternative estimator
decompositions \cite{bruno2004conditional}.
Cardinality Estimation Graphs represent
alternative complete summary-based paths
and study fixed or undefined path-selection
rules \cite{chen2022ceg}. Both are direct
precedents for competing estimator-side
applications, so ESS does not claim their
existence as a new phenomenon. These works
primarily construct or choose an estimator
path. ESS asks an \emph{upstream budgeted
installation} question: which resources
should be made available when doing so
induces \(\Gamma_i(M)\), but the advisor
cannot reliably predict the later choice?
Neither SIT decompositions nor CEG paths
are asserted to be PostgreSQL MCV matchings.

\paragraph{Ordering and complexity precedents.}
Our restricted hardness construction imports
the classical Feedback Arc Set / Maximum
Acyclic Subgraph problem
\cite{karp1972reducibility}; it does not
invent ordering hardness. Minimum maximal
matching and weighted greedy matching already
have established complexity and priority
results \cite{yannakakis1980edge,deligkas2017weighted}.
General decision-dependent uncertainty also
precedes ESS \cite{nohadani2018decision}.
Our contribution is the specific coupling
of estimator tasks by one global resource-edge
order. We do not claim generic precedence
feasibility as novel; full prior-art novelty
for the particular realizability test remains
to be established.

\paragraph{Estimation quality and robust decisions.}
PostgreSQL supports several extended
statistics kinds, including MCVs \cite{pg16stats}.
LpBound derives pessimistic \emph{cardinality
upper bounds} from degree statistics
\cite{zhang2025lpbound}; PESS instead
maximizes \emph{modeled loss} over complete
application schemes. PARQO and robust plan
coresets address plan choice under uncertain
estimates \cite{xiu2024parqo,raychaudhury2026coresets},
not installation under unknown estimator
application policies. Q-error is not a
surrogate for end-to-end plan quality
\cite{leis2015optimizers,han2022benchmark}.
AreCEL provides our workload family
\cite{wang2021arecel}, not the physical
estimator used in our experiments.

Taken together, these precedents locate
the novelty claim at the \emph{advisor--estimator
interface}, not at the discovery of
alternative estimates or robust optimization
in general. The comparison is targeted,
not an exhaustive prior-art survey.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
An advisor controls \emph{which statistics to install};
the estimator controls \emph{which available statistics
are applied}. ESS makes this information gap explicit
through resource-dependent complete scheme families and
objectives corresponding to different policy knowledge.
For one fixed resource set and aligned modeled loss,
\(A(M)\leq G(M)\leq P(M)\), and the taskwise PESS envelope
can be a tight factor of the task count above shared-order
risk. Shared-order feasibility is constructive when
target matchings are specified, while optimizing a common
order inherits classical hardness in a restricted model.
These are abstract, fixed-set results, not physical
Q-error guarantees.

The frozen Census13 Train witness shows that complete
schemes for the same task can have different modeled
losses. Conditional PostgreSQL 16.14 source reasoning and
controlled Test interventions exhibit physical order
sensitivity. At canonical order, B5 Nominal has lower
Test P95 in all four workloads; for the separate
counterfactual mean \emph{per-order} P95, PESS is favored
on Census13 and DMV11, Nominal on Forest10, and Power7
is inconclusive within its sampling interval.
Query-level diagnostics further show that changed MCV
application sequences need not change Q-error.
The two evaluations answer different policy questions;
neither establishes universal PESS superiority or
causal mediation by an individual MCV event.

For practice, a resource recommendation should
state its \emph{policy information assumption}:
a known priority, a specified distribution over shared
priorities, a worst shared priority, or independent
taskwise worst schemes. A better score under one assumption
does not establish superiority under another. PESS is a modeled
conservative envelope, but taskwise maxima need
not correspond to one jointly executable strategy.
Grounding the policy family in deployed behavior,
measuring maintenance and plan costs, and
improving outer selection are future work.
The present fixed-workload MCV evidence does not
supply those guarantees.
